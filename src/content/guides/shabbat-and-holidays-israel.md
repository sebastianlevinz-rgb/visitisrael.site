On Shabbat, from about 40 minutes before sunset on Friday until nightfall on Saturday, Israel's trains and buses stop, Jewish-owned shops and restaurants close and West Jerusalem goes quiet. It is not a lost day if you plan for it: the Western Wall at Friday dusk, a Shabbat dinner and a Saturday in the Old City are among the best experiences the country offers. This guide explains how Shabbat works, what changes in each city, how to use it well in Jerusalem, and which holidays in 2026 and 2027 do the same thing, or more.

Dates below were read from Hebcal (Israel calendar) on 23 September 2026. Opening hours are described in general terms and flagged where we could not confirm them on an official site; religious sites and transport operators publish holiday schedules a few days ahead, so check before you go.

## How Shabbat works

Shabbat is the Jewish day of rest, and in Israel it sets the weekly rhythm for everyone, observant or not. It begins on Friday evening and ends on Saturday night, which is why the Israeli weekend is Friday and Saturday and Sunday is a normal working day.

**When it starts.** Candle-lighting time, published every week, is 18 to 20 minutes before sunset in most of Israel and, by long-standing local custom, **40 minutes before sunset in Jerusalem**. The city is already closing an hour or two earlier: Mahane Yehuda market shutters by early afternoon, the light rail makes its last run before sunset, and the trains from Tel Aviv stop a couple of hours before that. Sunset in Jerusalem ranges from roughly 16:40 in December to 19:50 in late June, so a winter Shabbat starts mid-afternoon and a summer one after 19:00.

**The siren.** In Jerusalem a siren sounds across the city at candle-lighting time on Friday. It is not an alarm; it is the signal that Shabbat has begun, and one of the sounds people remember from the city.

**When it ends.** Shabbat ends at nightfall on Saturday, about 40 minutes after sunset. Buses and the light rail resume roughly an hour later, restaurants open by nine and, in Jerusalem as in Tel Aviv, Saturday night is one of the liveliest of the week.

**What "closed" means.** Observant Jews do not work, drive, cook or handle money on Shabbat. For the visitor: no public transport, no Jewish-owned shops, most kosher restaurants closed (hotel dining rooms serve pre-prepared meals), and hotel lifts that may run on an automatic "Shabbat" setting, stopping at every floor. Nothing stops you from walking, taking a taxi, eating in an Arab-owned or non-kosher restaurant, or visiting a church.

## How to spend a Shabbat in Jerusalem

Treat Friday afternoon and Saturday as one 24-hour unit and put the right things in the right slots.

### Friday

**Morning: Mahane Yehuda.** Friday is the market's busiest, best session: challah out of the ovens, families shopping for Shabbat, the halva and spice counters at full stretch. Eat lunch here; it is your last market meal until Saturday night. Stalls start closing from early afternoon, earlier in winter.

**Afternoon: watch the city change.** By mid-afternoon the light rail is on its last runs, Jaffa Road empties and West Jerusalem goes quiet street by street. Check in, shower and change now.

**Dusk: the Western Wall.** Walk to the Old City (Jaffa Gate is the easiest entry) and be on the plaza as the siren sounds. Over the next hour families, soldiers and yeshiva students arrive to pray and sing, with dancing in the men's section. The rules from sunset are strict and enforced: **no photography, no phones, no smoking**. Watch from the back of the plaza rather than the barrier, cover shoulders and knees, and men cover their heads (paper kippot are available). Do not film anyone at prayer.

**Evening: Shabbat dinner.** Three options. Many Jerusalem hotels, especially kosher ones, serve a Friday-night Shabbat dinner as a set sitting: book it with the room. Several organisations and families host visitors for a Shabbat meal, bookable ahead through tour platforms. Or eat in the Old City, East Jerusalem or the American Colony, where restaurants trade normally. Whatever you choose, reserve: the open restaurants are the only ones open.

### Saturday

**Morning: the Old City.** Enter through Jaffa Gate or New Gate and walk the Christian Quarter to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, which opens before dawn every day of the year and is calmest in the first hour. Continue into the Muslim Quarter: the souks along Souk Khan al-Zeit and al-Wad Street are fully open on Saturday and this is where the Old City eats. The Jewish Quarter is quiet and its archaeological sites are closed, but you can walk through it to the plaza steps for the classic view of the Wall and the Dome of the Rock. The Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif is not open to non-Muslims on Saturday.

**Late morning: the Mount of Olives.** Take a taxi to the top (the Seven Arches Hotel is the reference) and walk down past the lookout, Dominus Flevit, Gethsemane and the Church of All Nations to Lions' Gate. The churches close for services on Sunday rather than Saturday, so Saturday suits this walk.

**Afternoon: the museums that open.** The Israel Museum and the Tower of David Museum have historically kept Saturday hours, roughly late morning to late afternoon; we could not load their official pages on 23 September 2026, so confirm before relying on them. The Biblical Zoo, the Israel Aquarium and the Botanical Gardens are open and are the natural Shabbat plan for families. Taxis run all day at a weekend surcharge.

**Or leave the city.** Saturday is the day to give to a [Bethlehem](/jerusalem-bethlehem-day-trip) trip (by tour, taxi or the Arab buses from opposite Damascus Gate, which run on Saturday) or a Dead Sea day by [tour or rental car](/dead-sea-tours-compared). Neither works by Israeli public transport on Shabbat.

**Saturday night.** Within an hour of nightfall the light rail is moving, Ben Yehuda Street fills and the Mahane Yehuda bars open. If you have one big dinner to book in Jerusalem, this is the night.

## Jewish holidays 2026–2027: what closes and when

Jewish holidays follow a lunar-solar calendar, so their Gregorian dates move each year, and **every holiday begins at sunset on the evening before the date listed**. The key distinction for a traveller is whether a day is **Yom Tov**, a festival day on which the same restrictions as Shabbat apply: no public transport, shops and kosher restaurants closed, sites closed. In Israel the Yom Tov days are the two days of Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, the first and seventh days of Passover, the first day of Sukkot, Shemini Atzeret/Simchat Torah (one combined day in Israel) and Shavuot. The days in between during Passover and Sukkot (Chol HaMoed) are school holidays with shorter working hours: sites open, roads and hotels full. Purim, Hanukkah, the national days and Tisha B'Av change the mood but not the timetable.

## Christian and Muslim holidays

Jerusalem and Bethlehem run on three calendars, and the Christian and Muslim ones matter as much for the Old City as the Jewish one does for West Jerusalem.

**Holy Week and Easter 2027.** Western (Catholic and Protestant) Easter falls on **Sunday 28 March 2027**: Palm Sunday 21 March, Good Friday 26 March with the Franciscan procession on the Via Dolorosa. Orthodox Pascha is five weeks later, on **Sunday 2 May 2027**, with Orthodox Palm Sunday on 25 April, Good Friday on 30 April and the **Holy Fire ceremony on Holy Saturday, 1 May 2027**. In 2027 the gap is at its maximum, so Jerusalem has two separate pilgrim peaks; Old City and Christian-guesthouse rooms are booked out months ahead for both, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre is packed all week.

**Christmas in Bethlehem.** Bethlehem celebrates Christmas three times: **24–25 December** (Catholic and Protestant, with the Latin Patriarch's procession into Manger Square on the afternoon of the 24th and Midnight Mass in St Catherine's, screened on the square), **6–7 January** (Greek Orthodox and other Eastern churches) and **18–19 January** (Armenian). Midnight Mass is ticketed through the Latin Patriarchate and sells out; most visitors watch from the square. Jerusalem hotels fill around 24 December and the [Bethlehem day trip](/jerusalem-bethlehem-day-trip) needs booking ahead.

**Ramadan and the Eids.** Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin around **8 February** and end with **Eid al-Fitr around 9–10 March 2027**; Eid al-Adha is expected around **16 May 2027**. All three depend on the moon sighting, so allow a day either way. During Ramadan the Muslim Quarter is quiet by day and festive after dark, with Damascus Gate lit and food stalls open late; the **Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif is closed or heavily restricted for non-Muslim visitors** for the month and on both Eids (in 2026 police allowed limited morning visits, a change from earlier years; whether that repeats in 2027 is not known). In neither year does Ramadan overlap Passover or Holy Week.

## Three moments worth planning a trip around

**Yom Kippur: the day without cars.** From sunset on the eve until nightfall the next day, the whole country stops. Ben Gurion Airport suspends all flights from early afternoon on the eve (in 2025 the last Terminal 3 movements were just before 14:00) and reopens late the following evening, more than 30 hours later; land crossings close; radio and television go silent; and by unwritten national agreement nobody drives. Highways fill with children on bicycles and families on foot; in Jerusalem tens of thousands walk to the Western Wall for the closing Ne'ilah prayer. For the visitor: be where you want to be by midday on the eve with food bought (hotels serve pre-arranged meals; nothing else is open), rent a bicycle in advance in Tel Aviv, and spend the day walking. Yom Kippur 2027 begins at sunset on Sunday 10 October and ends at nightfall on Monday 11 October.

**Sukkot in Jerusalem.** For a week the city builds huts on balconies, in hotel gardens and outside restaurants, where meals are eaten under a roof of palm fronds. The intermediate days are open and festive. On two mornings of Chol HaMoed the **Priestly Blessing (Birkat Kohanim)** draws tens of thousands to the Western Wall plaza; in 2026 the Western Wall Heritage Foundation scheduled it for Monday 28 September and Thursday 1 October, with the blessing at about 09:30 and 10:30. Christian pilgrims come the same week for the Feast of Tabernacles and the Jerusalem March through the city centre. It is the most colourful week of the year to be in Jerusalem and the hardest to find a room.

**The Holy Fire.** On Orthodox Holy Saturday (1 May 2027) the Greek Orthodox Patriarch enters the sealed Tomb in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre and emerges with lit candles, which spread through the church and out to the crowd in seconds. Access is tightly controlled: in recent years police have capped numbers in and around the church and closed Old City streets from the morning, limits the churches have publicly disputed. Unless you are with a pilgrim group holding places, watch from the Christian Quarter rooftops or near New Gate, arrive very early, and expect the Old City to be hard to move through all day.

## Typical mistakes

- **Arriving at Ben Gurion on Friday evening without a plan.** Trains have stopped. The sherut to Jerusalem, taxis at a surcharge and the free line 711 to Tel Aviv all run, but a transfer booked ahead is the calm option.
- **Booking the Dead Sea or Galilee for a Saturday by bus.** There are no buses. Rent a car or book a tour.
- **Planning the Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif for Friday or Saturday.** It is closed to non-Muslim visitors both days every week, and for most of Ramadan.
- **Landing on Yom Kippur eve.** Flights stop in the early afternoon; a delayed morning arrival can strand you at the airport with no transport. Fly a day earlier.
- **Assuming a Jewish holiday is one day.** Rosh Hashanah is two, and whenever a Yom Tov adjoins Shabbat the closure runs two or three days.
- **Driving through Mea Shearim or Bnei Brak on Shabbat.** Streets are closed and residents object. Route around them.

## How to plan around Shabbat and the holidays

1. **Put the dates in your calendar first.** Before you fix flights, mark the Yom Tov days above and Yom Kippur. If your trip is a week, one Shabbat is unavoidable; make it the Jerusalem one.
2. **Book Jerusalem hotels early for Sukkot, Passover, Holy Week and Christmas.** Our [best hotels in Jerusalem](/best-hotels-jerusalem) guide names properties by neighbourhood; the German Colony and Mamilla are walkable to the Old City, which matters when nothing else is running. Ask whether the hotel serves Shabbat dinner and reserve it.
3. **Rent a car only for the Shabbat you want to travel on.** For a Saturday at the Dead Sea or in the Galilee a car is the practical option; the [car rental guide](/car-rental-israel) covers pick-up hours, tolls and the West Bank insurance limits. Collect it Friday morning.
4. **Book Saturday tours and Bethlehem in advance.** Tour operators run on Shabbat and their vehicles are the transport.
5. **Buy Friday food and use Saturday night.** Supermarkets and Mahane Yehuda close early Friday and reopen Saturday night, when restaurants and the light rail also return: book the good dinner for Saturday.
6. **First trip?** The [first time in Israel](/first-time-in-israel) guide covers money, dress codes and the Rav-Kav card you will need again on Sunday morning.
