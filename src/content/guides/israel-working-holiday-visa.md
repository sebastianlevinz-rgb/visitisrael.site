Israel's Working Holiday Visa is one of the more unusual things in the country's immigration landscape: a formal bilateral agreement that allows young adults from six specific countries to live and work legally in Israel for up to 12 months. Most Israel travel guides focus on tourist entry — the ETA-IL, the 90-day visitor permit, visa-exempt nationals. The Working Holiday Visa sits in a separate category entirely, and dedicated guidance on how it actually works is thin.

This guide covers the six eligible countries, the application process, what work is and is not permitted, and how to plan a year-long working stay in practical terms.

---

## The six eligible countries

Israel has signed Working Holiday Visa bilateral agreements with:

| Country | Max age at application | Max stay |
|---|---|---|
| Australia | 30 | 12 months |
| New Zealand | 30 | 12 months |
| Austria | 30 | 12 months |
| Germany | 30 | 12 months |
| Czech Republic | 30 | 12 months |
| South Korea | 30 | 12 months |

Age limits are set per bilateral agreement and apply at the time of application — not the time of arrival. Verify the exact upper age limit and any other country-specific conditions at the Israeli Embassy website for your country, as terms are occasionally updated. Citizens of countries not listed above are not eligible for a WHV; no other Israeli visa currently permits extended work.

---

## The core rules

Three rules govern every Israeli Working Holiday Visa:

**Travel must be the primary purpose.** An Israeli WHV is a travel visa with an incidental right to work — not a work permit with an incidental right to explore. Israeli immigration takes this distinction seriously. Visitors who clearly entered to work full-time with no travel component can face scrutiny at renewal or departure. In practice, most working holiday visitors do travel extensively between work stints; the rule is more a framing principle than a strict audit, but it is legally real.

**Maximum three months with any single employer.** You can work for as many employers as you like during 12 months, but no single employer for more than three consecutive months. This rule is designed to prevent WHV holders from functioning as ordinary long-term employees. In practice, it matches well with how seasonal agricultural and hospitality work is structured — a mango harvest in the Galilee, then a restaurant summer in Eilat, then a hotel winter season, each naturally runs three months or less.

**One WHV per agreement per person.** You are entitled to one Working Holiday Visa under each bilateral agreement over your lifetime. Once used, it cannot be re-applied for. There is no second WHV.

---

## How to apply

Applications are submitted at the Israeli Embassy or Consulate in your **home country** before departure. You cannot apply at an Israeli mission abroad or convert a tourist visa inside Israel.

Standard documents required (confirm the current list with the Embassy, as requirements occasionally change):

- Valid passport with at least 18 months of remaining validity from intended entry date
- Completed Israeli visa application form
- Recent passport photograph
- Proof of sufficient funds (a bank statement showing several thousand USD equivalent — exact amounts vary by bilateral agreement; confirm with the Embassy)
- Proof of onward or return travel or evidence of flexibility to leave at end of permit
- Travel health insurance covering the entire intended stay
- Application fee (amount varies by country — check the Embassy fee schedule)

Allow 6–8 weeks for processing, longer during peak periods. Some embassies accept postal applications; others require an in-person appointment. The Israeli Embassy websites for each eligible country list the current procedure.

## What you can work

**Agriculture** is the dominant sector for working holiday participants. The main harvests run across three main regions:

- **Galilee and Jordan Valley** — bananas, avocados, dates, mangoes (summer–autumn), apples, pears, grapes (autumn). Kibbutzim and private farms in the Upper Galilee and Jordan Valley employ seasonal workers and are accustomed to working holiday visitors. Kibbutzim sometimes provide basic accommodation in the package; private farms typically do not.
- **Eilot and Arava** (far south) — tomatoes, peppers, herbs, citrus, dates year-round due to the desert climate. The Arava region near Eilat employs a significant seasonal agricultural workforce. Hostels in Eilat and Yotvata serve as a staging ground.
- **Sharon Plain and coastal region** — citrus (winter), strawberries (spring), general vegetable cultivation.

**Hospitality** (hotels, hostels, restaurants, bars) is widely available in Tel Aviv, Jerusalem, and Eilat. Tel Aviv's high season runs June–September; Eilat's high season runs November–March. Approaching in person directly is generally more effective than online applications for short-term positions.

**English teaching** is available at private ulpan language schools and as private tutoring. Native-level English from an eligible country is enough to secure basic teaching work in Tel Aviv and Jerusalem.

## Planning 12 months in Israel

A working holiday gives you enough time to move through Israel's genuinely distinct regions — not just tick boxes but spend enough time that each place starts to feel familiar.

A rough seasonal sequence that many working holiday visitors follow:

**Tel Aviv base (months 1–2):** Arrive, get your bearings, open a bank account ([Pepper Bank](https://www.pepper.co.il) is an English-friendly digital bank popular with internationals), get an Israeli SIM. Find accommodation — the Florentin, Neve Tzedek and Levinsky market areas have hostels and short-let apartments popular with international workers. September–October has excellent warm-water beach weather.

**Northern Galilee agricultural work (months 3–5):** Autumn brings the main Galilee harvest season. Hostel stays in Tiberias, Rosh Pina, or Safed; farm day trips or live-in kibbutz placement. Side trips to the Golan Heights, Sea of Galilee, and the Lebanon-border north.

**Jerusalem immersion (months 6–7):** The holy city works best as an extended stay rather than a weekend trip. Hostels in the Christian Quarter, Nahalat Shiv'a, or Mount Zion area. Hospitality work is available; most importantly, 6–7 weeks is enough to begin to understand Jerusalem's layered geography — the Old City, the Markets, Mahane Yehuda, the German Colony, the Mount of Olives.

**Eilat winter season (months 8–10):** Eilat's resort economy peaks in winter (November–March). Hotel, bar, diving school, and watersports work; free diving and snorkeling at the Coral Beach Reserve is genuinely world-class. Day trips to the Negev — Ramon Crater, Timna Park, the Arabah desert.

**Negev and final stretch (months 11–12):** Road trip the Negev at your own pace — the Israel National Trail through the Negev is one of the world's great long-distance routes. Wind up with a return to Tel Aviv or wherever you most want to end.

## Practical setup on arrival

- **Bank account:** Pepper Bank or Discount Bank both serve English-speaking non-residents. Some documentation required — a rental agreement or hostel letter helps show a local address. Open within the first 2–3 weeks to receive wages via bank transfer.
- **SIM card:** Cellcom, Hot Mobile, and Pelephone all offer prepaid SIMs. For extended stays, a monthly SIM plan (₪40–80/month for unlimited data — verify current tariffs) is better value than top-up cards. See the [Israel eSIM guide](/israel-esim) for visitor data options.
- **Accommodation:** Tel Aviv furnished apartment shares run across Airbnb (for the first weeks), Facebook groups ("Tel Aviv Flats for Foreigners" is well-followed), and local agencies. Expect to pay a month's rent as deposit. Short-term furnished studios are available but at a premium.
