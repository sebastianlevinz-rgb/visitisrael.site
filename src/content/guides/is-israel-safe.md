**Short answer: yes, for most travellers, most of the time — with conditions.** Day-to-day crime in Israel is low, the main tourist cities are calm and heavily policed, and hundreds of thousands of visitors a year travel without incident. The real risk is not street crime but the regional security situation, which can change fast. As of 22 September 2026, the US, UK, Canadian and Australian governments all keep Israel under an elevated advisory ("reconsider travel" or "avoid non-essential travel") while stopping short of telling citizens not to go to Jerusalem, Tel Aviv or the Dead Sea. Below is what those advisories actually say, how the civil-defence system works if a siren sounds, and what the ordinary risks look like region by region.

## What the official travel advisories say (checked 22 September 2026)

Government advisories are the single most useful source, because they are updated within hours of a change and they distinguish between regions. They also matter for your insurance (see below). Here is where the four main English-language advisories stood when we checked them on 22 September 2026. Read the live page before you book and again before you fly; this summary will age.

**United States — Department of State.** Israel is at **Level 3: Reconsider Travel**, "due to terrorism and unrest". The advisory was renewed on 26 August 2026. Within that, the State Department lists **Do Not Travel** zones: within 11.3 km (7 miles) of the Gaza Strip, the far north (the area north of Route 85 towards the Lebanese border), and within 2.4 km of the Egyptian border except at the Taba crossing. The West Bank and Gaza carry their own, stricter guidance. Live page: [travel.state.gov — Israel, the West Bank and Gaza](https://travel.state.gov/content/travel/en/international-travel/International-Travel-Country-Information-Pages/IsraeltheWestBankandGaza.html). The [US Embassy in Jerusalem](https://il.usembassy.gov/) republishes each advisory and posts security alerts between updates.

**United Kingdom — FCDO.** The page was last updated on 22 July 2026 and was still current on 22 September 2026. The FCDO **advises against all travel** to Gaza, to within 500 m of the Gaza border, to the Tulkarm, Jenin and Tubas governorates of the West Bank (except Route 90), and to a strip of northern Israel north of Route 89 between Nahariya and Dalton plus specified zones of the Golan Heights near the Syrian and Lebanese borders. It **advises against all but essential travel** to the rest of the West Bank, with explicit exceptions for East Jerusalem, Bethlehem Governorate, Ramallah, Jericho and the highways linking them to Jerusalem. The rest of Israel — Jerusalem, Tel Aviv, Haifa, the Galilee below Route 89, the Dead Sea, the Negev and Eilat — is not under advice against travel, though the FCDO warns the situation "could escalate quickly". Live page: [gov.uk — Israel travel advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/israel).

**Canada — Global Affairs.** Updated 10 September 2026. Canada says **"Avoid non-essential travel"** to Israel, including Jerusalem, "due to the volatile security situation in the region", and **"Avoid all travel"** to the West Bank and Gaza. Regional advisories add: avoid all travel east of Highway 98 in the Golan Heights, within 5 km of the Lebanese border and within 10 km of the Gaza border. Live page: [travel.gc.ca — Israel and Palestine](https://travel.gc.ca/destinations/israel-and-palestine).

**Australia — Smartraveller.** The page carried a 2 September 2026 update in Google's index and, per that index, rates Israel **"Reconsider your need to travel"** (the third of four levels), with **"Do not travel"** to the border areas with Gaza, Lebanon and Syria. We could not load the page directly when we checked, so confirm the level yourself: [smartraveller.gov.au — Israel](https://www.smartraveller.gov.au/destinations/middle-east/israel).

Three things stand out across all four. First, none of them says "do not travel" to the places tourists actually go. Second, all four draw a hard line around the Gaza perimeter and the Lebanese and Syrian borders, and most of the northern West Bank. Third, all four explicitly warn that missile or rocket fire can happen anywhere in the country with little notice — which is why the next section matters more than any advisory level.

## How Israel's alert system works — sirens, shelters and the Home Front Command app

Israel has a national civil-defence system run by the IDF's Home Front Command (Pikud HaOref). Understanding it turns an abstract worry into a routine you can follow.

**The warning.** If a rocket or missile is fired towards a populated area, a rising-and-falling siren sounds in the specific zone that is threatened, not the whole country. At the same time, the official **Home Front Command app** (free, iOS and Android, English interface) pushes an alert to phones located in that zone and shows a countdown of how long you have to reach a protected space. Download it before you land, allow location access and notifications, and leave it installed for the whole trip. Several unofficial "Red Alert" apps exist; the Home Front Command's own app is the one that also carries official instructions.

**How much time you have.** The window depends on where you are, because it depends on distance from the launch point. Central Israel, including Jerusalem and Tel Aviv, gets roughly 90 seconds. Haifa gets about 30 seconds. Communities within a few kilometres of Gaza or Lebanon get around 15 seconds — which is one reason those areas sit under "do not travel" advisories. During the long-range missile exchanges of 2025–2026 the Home Front Command also issued advance "prepare" notifications minutes before the sirens themselves. The app shows the exact figure for your location.

**Where to go.** Every apartment built since the early 1990s has a reinforced room (a *mamad*); older buildings have a shared shelter or use an internal stairwell. Hotels have designated protected spaces and staff will direct you — ask where it is when you check in, the way you would ask about the fire exit. Public buildings, malls, train stations and light-rail stops have signed shelters. If you are outdoors with no building nearby, the official instruction is to lie flat on the ground, away from the road if possible, and cover your head with your hands. If you are driving, stop the car safely, get out, and move away from it to a protected space or lie flat.

**How long to stay.** The standing instruction is **ten minutes** after the siren, unless the app or an official announcement says otherwise. The wait is for falling debris from interceptions, not only for the missile itself. Then continue with your day: in most sirens experienced by tourists in Jerusalem or Tel Aviv, life resumes within the hour.

**In practice.** Most visits in 2026 involve no sirens at all. When they do happen, locals know the drill and will point you to the right place, and cafés, shops and hotels open their protected rooms to anyone. The Home Front Command also publishes, per region, whether gatherings are restricted; when it lifts restrictions, a flare-up has passed.

## Is Israel safe to visit, region by region?

Advisories are regional for good reason. Here is what applies where, as of September 2026, with the advisory zones above as the reference.

**Jerusalem.** Not under any "do not travel" advisory. The Old City and the main sights are policed heavily, with security checks at the Western Wall plaza and Temple Mount entrances. Sirens are rare but have happened. The specific cautions are situational: tension around the Temple Mount on Fridays and during religious holidays, and demonstrations that occasionally close streets. Follow police instructions, avoid crowds that form suddenly, and keep to well-trodden routes in East Jerusalem neighbourhoods if you are alone late at night. Our [Jerusalem guide](/jerusalem) covers the sights themselves.

**Tel Aviv and the coast.** Not under any "do not travel" advisory. The largest concentration of shelters and the most interception coverage in the country. Everyday risks are those of any beach city: bicycle and e-scooter traffic on the promenade, petty theft at the beach and in Carmel Market, and drink prices. See the [Tel Aviv guide](/tel-aviv).

**Dead Sea and Masada.** Not under advisory. The southern Dead Sea resort strip (Ein Bokek) and Masada are far from any border and see few or no sirens. The hazards here are environmental: heat, sun and, in winter, flash floods in the wadis. Details in our [Masada visitor guide](/masada-visitor-guide) and [Dead Sea page](/dead-sea).

**Galilee, Nazareth and the Sea of Galilee.** Nazareth, Tiberias, Capernaum and the Sea of Galilee shore are below the lines drawn by the UK and Canadian advisories and remain open to tourism. The far north — the strip along the Lebanese border north of Route 89, and Metula, Kiryat Shmona and the northern Golan — is under "do not travel" from every government listed above. Day tours from Tel Aviv and Jerusalem to the Galilee run to the lake and the Christian sites, not to the border. See the [Galilee guide](/galilee).

**Golan Heights.** Split. The southern Golan (Sea of Galilee lookouts, Katzrin wineries, the Gamla and Yehudiya reserves) is visited by tours; the areas east of Highway 98 and near the Syrian border are advised against by all four governments. Military closures can appear at short notice; if a road is closed, it is closed.

**Haifa and Akko.** Not under advisory, though Haifa has had a shorter warning time than the centre and was targeted during the 2024 and 2025 flare-ups. Check the app when you arrive. See the [Haifa guide](/haifa).

**Eilat and the Negev.** Eilat is the furthest point from the northern and Gaza fronts and was largely unaffected in 2025–2026, though it has seen occasional long-range drone or missile alerts. The Negev desert towns, Mitzpe Ramon and the Ramon Crater are not under advisory. The 2.4 km strip along the Egyptian border is, except at the Taba crossing. See the [Eilat](/eilat) and [Negev](/negev) guides.

**Bethlehem and the West Bank.** Bethlehem sits in the West Bank, about 10 km south of Jerusalem, and is the one West Bank destination that most tourists visit. The UK exempts Bethlehem Governorate, Ramallah and Jericho from its "all but essential" advice; the US and Canada advise against travel to the West Bank as a whole. Standard Israeli rental cars are not insured for Palestinian Authority areas, so nearly all visitors go on a guided tour or with a licensed local driver, and pass through an Israeli checkpoint each way with a passport. Our [Jerusalem and Bethlehem day trip](/jerusalem-bethlehem-day-trip) covers the logistics. Jenin, Nablus and the northern West Bank are advised against by everyone.

**Gaza and its perimeter.** Closed to tourism; "do not travel" from all four governments. Some operators run guided visits to the memorial sites near the Gaza border communities (the Nova festival site and the kibbutzim attacked in October 2023). These enter zones that the US and Canadian advisories cover; check your government's map and your insurance before booking one.

## Everyday risks: crime, roads, heat, water

Once the security question is set aside, the risks that actually affect tourists in Israel are the same ones as in Spain or Greece — and more likely to spoil a trip than anything in the news.

**Crime.** Violent crime against tourists is rare. Pickpocketing and bag theft happen where crowds do: the Old City lanes on a Friday, Mahane Yehuda and Carmel markets, the Tel Aviv beaches (do not leave a phone on a towel while you swim). Rental-car break-ins occur at trailheads and beach car parks; leave nothing visible. Taxi disputes are the most common tourist complaint: insist on the meter (*moneh*) or use Gett or Yango, where the fare is fixed in the app. See [car rental in Israel](/car-rental-israel) if you plan to drive.

**Roads.** Israeli driving is assertive, and road accidents are a bigger statistical risk to visitors than security incidents. Cyclists and e-scooters in Tel Aviv share pavements. Highway 90 along the Dead Sea and the desert roads to Eilat are long, straight and tempting — and heavily patrolled.

**Heat and sun.** From May to October, daytime temperatures in Jerusalem sit around 30 °C, in Tel Aviv slightly lower but humid, and at the Dead Sea and in the Arava well above 35 °C, sometimes over 40 °C. Heatstroke and dehydration are the commonest reason tourists end up in an Israeli emergency room. Carry more water than you think you need (two to three litres per person for a morning at Masada or in the Negev), start desert walks at dawn and be finished by late morning.

**Desert hiking.** The Negev and the Judean Desert have marked trail networks run by the Israel Nature and Parks Authority. Stay on them. Between October and April, rain in the Jerusalem hills produces flash floods in the Dead Sea wadis and Negev canyons hours later under a clear sky; the Parks Authority closes trails when the risk is high, and the closure is not optional. Tell your hotel where you are going, and never hike alone off-trail.

**Dead Sea.** Do not put your head under water — the water is about ten times as salty as the sea and burns the eyes and throat badly. Float on your back, keep the water away from your face and out of any cuts (shave the day before, not the day of). Wear water shoes: the salt crystals on the bottom are sharp. Most public beaches have freshwater showers; use them immediately. Stay on the maintained beaches at Ein Bokek and the official northern beaches — parts of the shoreline further north are closed because of sinkholes. Twenty minutes in the water is plenty.

**Mediterranean beaches.** Swim at lifeguarded beaches during lifeguard hours and watch the flags: white means safe, red means caution, black means no swimming. Rip currents are the main hazard on the Tel Aviv–Herzliya coast. Jellyfish arrive in numbers most years in July and August. The Sea of Galilee has strong afternoon winds and cold currents in places despite looking like a lake.

**Diving and snorkelling in Eilat.** The Red Sea reefs off Eilat are accessible from the shore and the dive centres are well regulated. Dive with a licensed operator, do not touch the coral (it is protected by law), and leave 18–24 hours before flying. The Coral Beach Nature Reserve has marked snorkelling routes.

**Health.** Tap water is safe. Healthcare is high-standard and expensive for non-residents without insurance. Pharmacies are everywhere and pharmacists speak English. Emergency numbers: **100** police, **101** ambulance (Magen David Adom), **102** fire.

## Safety for solo women, LGBTQ+ travellers and families

**Women travelling alone.** Israel is a straightforward destination for solo women. Public transport, taxis by app and walking in city centres after dark are routine. The adjustments are cultural rather than security-related: cover shoulders and knees at holy sites and in ultra-Orthodox neighbourhoods such as Mea Shearim in Jerusalem, where signs ask visitors to dress modestly, and expect a more conservative atmosphere in some Arab towns and in East Jerusalem than in Tel Aviv. Persistent harassment in markets is uncommon but not unknown; a firm "no" ends it.

**LGBTQ+ travellers.** Tel Aviv is one of the most openly gay-friendly cities in the region, with a large Pride event in June and a visible scene year-round. Same-sex couples face no legal issues anywhere in the country. Public displays of affection draw attention in religious areas of Jerusalem and in conservative towns, for any couple; use judgment there as you would in a village church in southern Europe.

**Families.** Israel is a very child-oriented society: restaurants welcome children, hotels routinely offer family rooms, and museums and nature reserves are built for school groups. Two practical notes. The Old City of Jerusalem is steps and cobbles, so a carrier beats a stroller. And talk to older children about the siren drill in the same matter-of-fact way you would explain a fire alarm at school; Israeli children learn it in kindergarten, and framing it as a routine removes most of the fear.

## Travel insurance for Israel: what to look for

Insurance is where the advisory level bites, so read the policy wording rather than the brochure.

**The advisory exclusion.** Most standard policies from UK, EU and Australian insurers exclude claims arising in a place where your government advises against all travel or all but essential travel. Because the FCDO does not advise against travel to the main tourist areas, a UK policy will typically cover Jerusalem, Tel Aviv and the Dead Sea, but not Jenin or the Gaza perimeter. Canadian policies may treat "avoid non-essential travel" as an exclusion for the whole country — check before you buy. US policies rarely use the State Department level as a trigger, but they do exclude "war" and sometimes "acts of terrorism"; look for a policy that names terrorism as a covered event.

**What the policy should cover.** Emergency medical treatment and evacuation (the essential part); trip cancellation and interruption; and, if the price is reasonable, a "cancel for any reason" upgrade, which is the only type that refunds you if you simply decide not to go after a flare-up. Some specialist insurers sell policies specifically for destinations under an elevated advisory; they cost more, and they are the right product if you are travelling against your government's general advice.

**Airline and hotel flexibility** is a second layer. Book fares that allow changes, and hotels with free cancellation up to a day or two before arrival. In each of the 2024–2026 disruptions, the visitors who had the least trouble were the ones whose bookings could be moved.

## What to do if the situation changes during your trip

Flare-ups in 2024, 2025 and 2026 followed a recognisable pattern, and tourists who were in the country describe the same sequence.

**Flights.** Foreign airlines tend to suspend Israel routes first and resume them last; the Israeli carriers (El Al, Arkia, Israir) have historically kept flying through most disruptions, and Ben Gurion has stayed open except for short closures. If your carrier cancels, contact it immediately for rebooking or a refund, and check whether an Israeli carrier has seats. Fares rise fast in those windows. Your insurer may cover the difference under trip interruption.

**Land exits.** If Ben Gurion closes, there are three land crossings to Jordan — the Allenby/King Hussein Bridge near Jericho, the Sheikh Hussein (Jordan River) crossing in the north, and the Yitzhak Rabin/Wadi Araba crossing at Eilat — and the Taba crossing to Egypt. Amman has an international airport. Both sides charge border fees and Jordanian visa rules apply. During the March 2026 disruption the Israeli Ministry of Tourism organised shuttles for stranded visitors, with the details relayed through embassy alerts — a sign that a structure exists, not a guarantee it will run next time. Check our [visa information](/visa-information) for entry rules if you re-enter.

**Your embassy.** Register before you travel: US citizens with STEP, Britons by signing up for FCDO email alerts on the Israel page, Canadians with Registration of Canadians Abroad, Australians through Smartraveller subscriptions. Embassies rarely evacuate tourists when commercial options exist, but they are the source of accurate instructions, and the US Embassy in Jerusalem, for one, posts security alerts within hours.

**On the ground.** Follow Home Front Command restrictions on gatherings; if a region is under restrictions, tours there are cancelled and hotels in the area will tell you. In past disruptions hotels have generally extended the stays of guests who could not leave; ask at reception early rather than late. Keep your phone charged, your passport on you, and cash for a day or two in case card networks are busy.

**Cancelling before you go.** If a flare-up starts before departure: check whether your airline has cancelled (a cancellation means a refund regardless of insurance); check whether your government's advice has changed to "against all travel" for your destinations (that triggers most European policies); then talk to your insurer. Free-cancellation hotels can simply be cancelled, and tours booked through the big platforms usually carry 24-hour free cancellation.

## Pre-trip safety checklist

- Read your government's advisory page for Israel when you book, the week you fly, and the day you fly.
- Register with your embassy's traveller programme.
- Buy insurance and read the exclusions for government advisories and terrorism.
- Download the Home Front Command app before departure; enable location and notifications.
- Book flexible fares and free-cancellation hotels for at least the first and last nights.
- Ask at hotel check-in where the protected space is.
- Save 100 / 101 / 102 and your embassy's number in your phone.
- Carry your passport (or a photo of it and the entry slip) for checkpoints, some sites and the airport.
- Pack for the environment: hat, sunscreen, water bottle, water shoes for the Dead Sea, cover-up for holy sites.
- Keep out of advised-against zones; a reputable tour operator will not take you into one.

## The bottom line

Israel in 2026 is a country where the ordinary traveller's risks — sun, roads, pickpockets — are low to moderate and easily managed, and where the extraordinary risk — regional conflict — is real, geographically concentrated, and covered by a civil-defence system that works. Whether that balance is right for you depends on your own tolerance, and that is a decision to make with your government's current advisory in one hand and a flexible booking in the other.
