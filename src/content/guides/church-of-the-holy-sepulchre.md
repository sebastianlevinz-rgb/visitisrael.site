The Church of the Holy Sepulchre is free, open before dawn every day of the year, and busiest between mid-morning and mid-afternoon. If you want to stand inside the Aedicule, the small shrine that encloses the Tomb, go in the first hour after the doors open; by 10:00 the queue for it regularly runs past an hour. Everything else on this page follows from those two facts.

This guide covers what is inside the church site by site, the hours as three separate sources state them and where they disagree, how long the Tomb queue runs at different times of day, what to wear, the Catholic Mass schedule with its languages and the office a group books through, what the sharing of the building actually changes for a visitor, and the walk in from each of the Old City gates. Hours and Mass times below were read on 23 September 2026 and are cited to the page they came from; religious sites move their schedules around feasts, so confirm the week you travel.

## What is inside, site by site

From the outside the church reads as one dim building. Inside it is a stack of separate places on three levels, most of them reached by stairs, and knowing the order saves you from walking past half of them.

**The Parvis.** The stone courtyard in front of the single door, reached from the Muristan or from Christian Quarter Road. The bell tower is on your left as you face the entrance; the small Chapel of the Franks, with its own outside staircase, is on your right and is usually closed.

**The Stone of Anointing.** A slab of reddish limestone set in the floor immediately inside the door, under a row of hanging lamps. The present stone dates from 1810. Visitors kneel and press cloths and rosaries to it; you can walk around it without queueing.

**Calvary, or Golgotha.** Up the steep, worn staircase to your right as you enter, at about five metres above the church floor. Two chapels share the platform: the Greek Orthodox Chapel of the Crucifixion, with a silver-clad altar and an opening beneath it through which visitors reach down to touch the rock, and the Catholic Chapel of the Nailing to the Cross beside it. Between them is the Catholic altar of Our Lady of Sorrows. The staircase is one-way in practice when the church is busy; you come down the second flight on the far side.

**The Rotunda and the Aedicule.** The great domed hall on the west side, with the Aedicule standing free in the middle. You enter it in two steps: first the Chapel of the Angel, then, through a very low doorway, the tomb chamber itself, which fits three or four people at a time. The whole structure was dismantled, cleaned and re-pinned in a restoration completed in 2017. This is the only part of the church with a queue.

**The Katholikon.** The Greek Orthodox choir under the second dome, in the middle of the building, with a stone omphalos in the floor and a tall iconostasis. Open to walk through outside service times.

**The Chapel of the Apparition.** The Franciscan chapel on the north side of the Rotunda, and the only corner of the church that is usually calm. The daily Franciscan procession starts here.

**The Chapel of St Helena.** Down a long flight of 29 worn steps from the ambulatory behind the Katholikon, lined with crosses carved into the wall by medieval pilgrims. The chapel is Armenian, low and vaulted, with mosaic floors laid in the twentieth century.

**The Chapel of the Finding of the Cross.** Down a further flight from St Helena, cut into bare rock; the lowest point you can reach. Adjacent is the Chapel of St Vartan, an Armenian space opened only on request.

**The Ethiopian monastery, Deir es-Sultan.** A cluster of cells and two small chapels on the roof of the St Helena chapel, reached not from inside the church but from a separate door off the courtyard beside it. It is a short, strange, quiet detour and most visitors never find it.

A sensible circuit, allowing sixty to ninety minutes and ignoring the Tomb queue: Stone of Anointing, up to Calvary, down the far stairs, Rotunda and Aedicule, Katholikon, Chapel of the Apparition, then down to St Helena and the Finding of the Cross, and out through the courtyard to the Ethiopian roof.

## Opening hours and the first-hour rule

**Admission is free and there are no closed days.** The church opens before dawn and closes in the evening, every day of the year, and that is the part every source agrees on.

The exact times are less settled than the internet suggests. We could not load an official Custody of the Holy Land hours page on 23 September 2026, and the three secondary sources we did read give three different schedules:

- `seetheholyland.net` lists opening at 04:00 from April to September and 05:00 from October to March, closing at 20:00 from April to August, 19:30 in March and September and 19:00 from October to February.
- `churchoftheholysepulchre.net` lists 05:00 to 20:00 from April to September and 05:00 to 19:00 from October to March.
- `thechurchoftheholysepulchre.com` lists 05:00 to 20:45 in summer, 05:00 to 20:00 in October, 04:00 to 19:00 from November to February and 04:00 to 19:30 in March.

The honest summary is a range: **the doors open somewhere between 04:00 and 05:00 and close somewhere between 19:00 and 20:45, by season.** Anyone publishing a single confident timetable for this church is copying one of those three pages. Nothing here is ticketed or timed, so getting it wrong by an hour costs you a coffee at Jaffa Gate, not an entry.

**The first-hour rule.** Whatever the opening minute, the hour after it is the only hour of the day the basilica is close to empty. Coach groups reach the Old City from mid-morning; from roughly 09:30 to 15:00 the church is full and loud, and the Rotunda in particular becomes a slow shuffle. The hour before closing is the second-best slot and has the advantage that you do not have to be awake at four. This is the single reason to sleep inside the walls or immediately outside Jaffa Gate or New Gate: the walk in at 05:00 is five to ten minutes, and from a hotel in the German Colony it is a taxi you have to book the night before.

The door is unlocked and locked each day by a member of one Jerusalem family carrying the key and another opening the lock, an arrangement that has run for centuries and takes a few minutes at the entrance. If you arrive early enough you will see it; nobody is turned away for watching.

## How long you wait for the Tomb

Only the Aedicule has a queue. The rest of the church you walk into.

| When you arrive | What to expect at the Aedicule |
| --- | --- |
| First hour after opening | A few minutes, often none at all. The reliable slot. |
| Mid-morning onward (from about 09:30) | `churchoftheholysepulchre.net` states the wait reaches about an hour. Group arrivals set the rhythm, so it varies by the day of the week. |
| Early to mid afternoon | The longest of the day in high season. Coaches are still arriving and many groups take Calvary first, then the Tomb. |
| Last hour before closing | Shorter again, but a liturgy may close the Aedicule entirely for part of it. |
| Holy Week and Orthodox Holy Week | Longer than any of the above, and the church is sometimes closed to general visitors for ceremonies. |

Two things nobody warns you about. First, **the queue stops** whenever a liturgy is celebrated inside the Tomb, which happens several times a day for a few minutes at a time; the line does not move and then moves fast. Second, **you get about thirty seconds inside the tomb chamber** when it is busy, and a guardian will move you along. Decide before you go in whether you are there to look or to photograph; you will not do both well.

If the queue is long and your time is short, the Chapel of the Apparition, Calvary and the Chapel of St Helena are all worth more of your attention than an hour standing in the Rotunda, and you can come back the next morning at opening.

## What to wear

Shoulders and knees covered, for men and women. Unlike the Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif, **the Holy Sepulchre does not lend wraps at the door**, so a scarf, a light shirt or a long skirt in your bag is the difference between going in and not. Men remove hats inside. Sleeveless tops, shorts above the knee and beachwear will get you turned back at the entrance in summer, which is exactly when you are most likely to be wearing them.

Two practical notes that are not dress code but belong here: the floors are polished, uneven stone and both staircases are steep and often wet from cleaning, so wear shoes with a grip; and the interior is genuinely dark, so if you have trouble with steps in low light, take Calvary before your eyes are tired and hold the handrail.

## Mass at the Holy Sepulchre: times, languages and how a group books

This is the question the travel sites do not answer, so here is what the Custody of the Holy Land publishes itself, read on `cicts.org/en/catholic-masses-jerusalem-0` on **23 September 2026**.

**Catholic Masses in the basilica, summer schedule**

- Sundays: Masses on Calvary from 06:00 to 08:00, roughly every half hour, with the language varying by slot; a Mass at the Tomb at 06:00; Latin High Mass at 06:30.
- Weekdays: Masses on Calvary from 06:00 to 08:00; Latin High Mass at 07:30, every day except Friday.
- Franciscan procession inside the basilica: 17:00, daily.

**Winter schedule**

- Sundays: Calvary from 05:00 to 07:00; Mass at the Tomb at 05:00; Latin High Mass at 05:30.
- Weekdays: Calvary from 05:00 to 07:00; Latin High Mass at 06:30, except Friday.
- Franciscan procession: 16:00, daily.

**The detail that changes everything.** The Custody's own listing states plainly that **if there is no group booking, there is no Mass** in the Calvary slots. Those early-morning times are a framework of reservable slots, not a fixed public timetable. What runs regardless is the Latin High Mass and the afternoon procession. So: an individual traveller who simply wants to attend Mass here should aim for the High Mass or the procession; a pilgrimage group that wants its own Mass at a specific altar has to reserve one.

**How a group books.** Through the **Franciscan Pilgrims Office** at Jaffa Gate (Omar Ibn el-Qattab Square, in the same building as the Christian Information Centre). It is the office that allocates Mass slots in the Custody's sanctuaries across the Holy Land, and it does not sell tours or packages. Contact details as published on `cicts.org`: **fpo@cicts.org**, **+972 2 627 2697**, open **Monday to Friday 09:00 to 13:00**, closed Saturday, Sunday and Christian holidays. The basilica's own numbers are 02 626 7000 for the convent and 02 626 7011 for the sacristy, and the sacristy is who you contact about an overnight vigil inside the church. Every operator that promises you "daily Mass" on a Holy Land itinerary is going through this office; asking a tour company which sanctuaries and which times it actually holds is a fair and revealing question.

**Nearby, if the basilica does not fit your day.** The same Custody listing gives Mass in other Jerusalem churches, with their working languages: Notre Dame Center in English (Sundays 09:00 and 18:30, weekdays 18:30), St Anne's by Lions' Gate in French (Sundays 07:00, weekdays 06:45), St Stephen's in French (Sundays 07:00 and 11:30, weekdays 12:00), and the Basilica of the Agony at Gethsemane in Italian (Sundays 07:00 and 16:00, weekdays 06:30).

Greek Orthodox, Armenian, Coptic and Syriac liturgies also run in the basilica, several of them in the small hours, and visitors may stand and watch. Those schedules are not published in one place; the sacristy or your guide will know the day's pattern.

## Six communities under one roof: what it changes for your visit

The building is administered by three communities together, the Greek Orthodox Confraternity of the Holy Sepulchre, the Franciscan Custody of the Holy Land and the Armenian Brotherhood of St James, with Coptic, Syriac and Ethiopian communities holding their own chapels. That arrangement is the reason the church looks and behaves the way it does, and four consequences are purely practical.

**Liturgies run all day, in several places at once.** You will often hear two services from different traditions overlapping, and a procession may cross the Rotunda while you are in it. None of this is a closure; you step aside and it passes. It also means the church is never the silent space its photographs suggest.

**Access to individual chapels is not uniform.** Each community keeps its own hours for its own spaces. The Chapel of the Franks on the outside staircase, the Chapel of St Vartan below St Helena and a few side altars are opened on request or not at all. If a door is locked, that is normal and not worth chasing.

**The Ethiopian rooftop has its own entrance.** Deir es-Sultan is reached from the courtyard, not from inside the basilica, and keeps daytime hours of its own. Most visitors miss it because there is no sign pointing to it from the nave.

**The liturgical calendars are different, and so are the crowds.** Western Easter and Orthodox Easter can fall five weeks apart, as they do in 2027, when Western Easter is 28 March and Orthodox Pascha is 2 May. That produces two separate pilgrim peaks in the same spring rather than one, and during both the church is at its fullest and sometimes restricted. Our [Shabbat and holidays guide](/shabbat-and-holidays-israel) has the 2026 to 2027 dates for all three calendars, including the Holy Fire ceremony on 1 May 2027.

That is the whole of what the shared administration means for a visitor. The history behind it is long and much written about elsewhere; it makes no difference to how you spend ninety minutes here.

## Getting there from each gate

The church has **one public entrance**, on the south side, opening onto the Parvis. Every route below ends there. The Old City is a twenty-minute walk end to end, so none of these is long; the difficulty is the turns.

- **Jaffa Gate** — the default. Straight ahead into David Street, then left onto Christian Quarter Road and follow it north; the church is signposted right, down into the courtyard. Seven to ten minutes. This is the gate to use from the light rail (City Hall stop), Mamilla and most hotels.
- **New Gate** — the shortest walk of all, about five minutes, and the quietest. Straight in, then right and down through the Christian Quarter. This is the gate for the East Jerusalem hotels on Nablus Road and Salah ad-Din Street.
- **Damascus Gate** — down the steps, take Souk Khan al-Zeit, the busier of the two souks, and walk south for about eight minutes until a left-hand flight of steps marks the Ninth Station; the entrance to the church courtyard is a little further on, to the right. Crowded and the easiest route to overshoot. Ten to twelve minutes.
- **Lions' Gate** — this is the Via Dolorosa itself, from the First Station to the Ninth and then into the church. Fifteen to twenty minutes walking, an hour and a half or more if you stop at the stations. See our [Via Dolorosa guide](/via-dolorosa) for the route station by station.
- **Herod's Gate** — into the residential Muslim Quarter, then join al-Wad Street or Souk Khan al-Zeit southbound. About twelve minutes and easy to get lost on the first attempt.
- **Dung Gate** — the gate for the Western Wall plaza and the tour-bus car park. Up through the Jewish Quarter and the Cardo, then into the Muristan. Fifteen minutes and uphill most of the way.
- **Zion Gate** — through the Armenian Quarter to David Street, then as from Jaffa Gate. Twelve minutes.

**What to combine it with.** The Via Dolorosa arrives here, which makes the classic Jerusalem morning a single line: the Mount of Olives at first light, down through Gethsemane to Lions' Gate, the Via Dolorosa, and the Holy Sepulchre before the coaches. The Lutheran Church of the Redeemer is next door and its bell tower gives the clearest view down onto the church's domes. The Western Wall is ten minutes away through the souk. **If you have half a day and want it explained**, a licensed guide is worth more here than at almost any other site in the country, because nothing inside is labelled and the layout hides most of itself.
