Kibbutz volunteering was one of the defining travel experiences of the 20th century — a chance for young international visitors to live inside a working Israeli collective, contribute physical labour, eat communally, and immerse in Hebrew. The movement has evolved: most kibbutzim are now privatised to varying degrees, and the era of pure communal idealism is historical. But the core offer remains intact — **free accommodation, meals, and a genuine window into Israeli rural life** in exchange for honest work.

Israel hosts several structured volunteering pathways in 2026, from free MASA Israel programs for Jewish diaspora youth to the five-month Kibbutz Ulpan language immersion course to independent self-placement. This guide covers how each path works, what the application process looks like, what daily life actually involves, and the visa requirements that differ from a standard tourist visit.

For information on simply staying at a kibbutz hotel without volunteering, see the [kibbutz hotel guide](/kibbutz-hotels-israel). For long-term accommodation options more broadly, see the [Israel accommodation guide](/israel-accommodation-guide).

## The four volunteering pathways

### 1. MASA Israel Kibbutz Program (free; Jewish diaspora)

MASA Israel (a partnership of the Jewish Agency and Israeli government) runs subsidised programs for Jewish diaspora participants aged 18–30. The kibbutz-specific program provides free flights, accommodation, meals, and Hebrew classes for cohorts of 5–8 weeks. Participants work 25–30 hours per week on agricultural or facility tasks and attend Hebrew and Israel-culture sessions the rest of the time.

**Eligibility:** Jewish heritage (you, a parent, or a grandparent) or a Jewish spouse. Israeli citizens are not eligible. Most programs require at least a high-school diploma.

**Cost:** Free for the participant. MASA funds accommodation, meals, and activities and subsidises the flight (reimbursement model — pay upfront and receive the subsidy on arrival or completion).

**How to apply:** Apply at masaisrael.org/en. The summer cohort (June–August) is the most popular; apply 3–4 months ahead. Kibbutz placement is assigned, not chosen, though regional preferences are considered.

---

### 2. Kibbutz Ulpan (5 months; Hebrew immersion)

The Kibbutz Ulpan program has operated since 1962 and is the most structured long-term option. Participants spend five months on a specific kibbutz, working 25 hours per week in exchange for accommodation and meals, and attending Hebrew language school (ulpan) for 25 hours per week. Classes run at beginner through advanced levels. Start dates are October and March.

**Eligibility:** Open to non-Jewish applicants with a genuine interest in Hebrew language learning. Jewish applicants are also welcome. Age range is typically 18–35 in practice, though not formally capped.

**Cost:** A registration and ulpan fee applies (check kibbutzulpan.org for current rates). Accommodation and meals are included. You pay your own flights.

**How to apply:** Apply directly to a specific participating kibbutz via kibbutzulpan.org, which lists kibbutzim with active ulpan programs and their intake dates. Apply at least 3–4 months before the start date. You need a **B/4 Volunteer Visa** — the kibbutz coordinator typically guides this process.

**What you'll learn:** Hebrew at 25 hours per week for five months produces genuine functional literacy. Beginners consistently reach daily-life proficiency. For some participants, Kibbutz Ulpan is a precursor to Aliyah (immigration to Israel).

---

### 3. The Kibbutz Program (independent placements)

The original Kibbutz Program now operates through a network of Israeli consulates and independent agents. Stays run 1–3 months; participants are placed on a kibbutz, work 6–8 hours per day six days a week, and receive accommodation, meals, and a small pocket allowance (typically ₪100–300/week — confirm current rates as they change).

**Eligibility:** Generally open, not restricted to Jewish applicants. Minimum age 18.

**How to find placements:** Contact Volunteer World (volunteerworld.com) or Go Overseas (gooverseas.com), or use the kibbutz volunteer section of the Jewish Agency's international website. Some kibbutzim list openings directly on their own websites.

**Visa:** For stays under 90 days, the standard ETA-IL tourist authorisation is usually sufficient. For stays approaching or exceeding 90 days, apply for a B/4 Volunteer Visa through your nearest Israeli embassy.

---

### 4. Self-placement (direct contact)

Experienced travellers or those who want maximum flexibility contact kibbutzim directly. This works best for kibbutzim with hotel or tourism facilities, which regularly need additional hands.

**Who to contact:** A kibbutz guesthouse manager or volunteer coordinator. Search for the kibbutz name with "volunteer" to find contact details. The kibbutz.org.il directory lists member kibbutzim.

**When to apply:** August–September (harvest), March–May (spring agriculture), and holiday periods (Sukkot, Passover) are peak demand times when kibbutzim are most receptive to volunteer inquiries.

## What daily kibbutz life is like

The romanticised image of the kibbutz as a pure socialist commune is largely historical. Most Israeli kibbutzim privatised (became "kibbutz mitchaddesh") between 2000 and 2015: members now receive differential salaries, private housing is owned rather than collectively shared, and children sleep in family homes. The communal dining hall may now charge per meal rather than being free for all members.

For volunteers, the practical experience still includes:

**Communal dining hall.** Three meals a day in the shared dining room — breakfast, a larger lunch, and a lighter dinner. The food is plentiful, institutional in character, and often includes fresh produce grown on-site. Dietary requirements (vegetarian, kosher) are generally accommodated; vegan options vary by kibbutz.

**Shared accommodation.** Volunteers typically share rooms in a dedicated volunteer house or converted accommodation separate from the main residential area. The standard is functional — expect a shared bathroom with other volunteers.

**Six-day working week.** Friday afternoon to Saturday night (Shabbat) is the standard rest period. On Shabbat, the dining hall often serves a formal Shabbat dinner Friday evening — one of the most authentic cultural experiences available to volunteers.

**Social life.** Most kibbutzim have a small volunteer social scene, a pool (if the kibbutz has one), and a social club. The social environment depends on how many other international volunteers are present at the same time — ask the volunteer coordinator about typical volunteer numbers before committing.

**Hebrew.** You do not need Hebrew to volunteer. English is widely understood by kibbutz members in their 20s–50s. Basic Hebrew phrases make daily interactions smoother — see the [Israel Hebrew phrases guide](/israel-hebrew-phrases) for the essentials.

## Visa and entry requirements

**ETA-IL (all visa-exempt nationalities):** Required before arrival, regardless of volunteering status or duration. Apply at israel-entry.piba.gov.il; fee ₪25 (~$7); valid ~2 years / multiple entries; apply at least 72 hours before departure. For the full entry requirements framework, see the [visa information guide](/visa-information) and the [Israel ETA guide](/israel-eta-guide).

**B/2 tourist stay (under 90 days):** Informal short volunteering — self-placement, short program — on a tourist entry is generally accepted for stays under 90 days. The pocket allowance arrangement of approved volunteer programs is distinct from paid employment and does not require a work visa.

**B/4 Volunteer Visa (over 90 days, or formal programs):** Required for the Kibbutz Ulpan and MASA Israel programs. Apply through the Israeli Embassy or Consulate in your home country. The visa must be issued before you arrive; it cannot be obtained in Israel. Apply 6–8 weeks before your program start date. Your kibbutz or program organiser will provide a letter of invitation.

**Travel insurance:** Required by most formal programs and strongly recommended for all stays. Your home health insurance does not cover you in Israel. See [Israel travel insurance](/israel-travel-insurance) for options — SafetyWing Nomad Insurance is widely used by kibbutz volunteers for its affordable monthly cost.

## Getting to your kibbutz

Kibbutzim are spread across the country — Upper Galilee, Jordan Valley, Negev, and coastal areas are the main concentrations. Most are not on public transit routes.

- **Organised transfer:** MASA Israel and Kibbutz Ulpan programs typically arrange group transport from Ben Gurion Airport to the kibbutz for orientation day arrivals.
- **Bus + sherut (shared taxi):** Most major kibbutzim are reachable from the nearest city (Tiberias for the Sea of Galilee area, Be'er Sheva for the Negev, Haifa for the Haifa Bay) by Egged or Dan bus; from there, a shared taxi covers the final stretch.
- **Car rental:** For self-placement volunteers who need flexibility, renting a car for the first and last few days is useful. Compare rates via the [car rental Israel guide](/car-rental-israel).

## Costs and budget

The kibbutz volunteer arrangement covers your two largest expenses — accommodation and food. Remaining out-of-pocket costs:

| Item | Estimated range |
|------|----------------|
| Return flight | ₪1,500–₪4,000 ($400–$1,100) depending on origin and season |

| B/4 Volunteer Visa (if required) | ~₪250 ($70) at embassy |
