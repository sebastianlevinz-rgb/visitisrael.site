Simchat Torah arrives at the very end of the High Holiday season — the day after Sukkot ends — and it lands like an exhale. The solemn introspection of Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur is weeks in the past; the week-long tabernacle hospitality of Sukkot is complete. What follows is pure joy: the Torah scrolls come out of the ark, the music starts, and the dancing begins.

In Israel, Simchat Torah is celebrated on the same day as Shemini Atzeret (the day immediately following Sukkot), meaning the full holiday falls on **a single day: October 3, 2026**, with evening celebrations beginning at nightfall on Friday, October 2. Because October 3 coincides with Shabbat this year, the public celebrations are especially concentrated — and Jerusalem's transport situation mirrors a Shabbat closure.

For visitors who have timed a trip around Sukkot, staying one extra night to witness Simchat Torah is among the most worthwhile decisions they can make. It is one of the most accessible and welcoming Jewish celebrations for non-Jewish visitors: joyful, public, and built around music that spills into the streets.

## What is Simchat Torah?

The Jewish Torah reading cycle runs through the entire Five Books of Moses over the course of a year, read weekly in synagogues in prescribed portions. On Simchat Torah, the final portion (the end of Deuteronomy) is read and immediately the cycle begins again with the opening of Genesis — completing one cycle and beginning another in the same moment.

The central ritual is the **hakafot**: seven joyful circuits around the synagogue bimah, Torah scrolls held aloft, accompanied by singing and dancing. In large congregations the scrolls are passed between members; in street celebrations they move through outdoor processions. The word *hakafah* means "circuit" — the scrolls circle, the congregation dances, and the cycle of Torah reading circles back to its beginning.

In 2026, Simchat Torah falls on Shabbat (Saturday), which means the daytime is a double observance — both Shabbat and Simchat Torah. The **evening celebrations on Friday, October 2** are the primary event for visitors: this is when the streets of Mea She'arim fill with music and dancing, when the Western Wall plaza is at its most festive, and when Dizengoff Square comes alive in Tel Aviv.

Public transport note: Jerusalem's buses stop for Shabbat from Friday afternoon; the light rail and buses resume Saturday night after nightfall. Plan transport in Jerusalem accordingly — walk to nearby celebration sites, or book accommodation in the Old City or Mea She'arim area for walkability. In Tel Aviv, private transport services operate on Friday evenings and the light rail runs through the night.

## The Western Wall plaza

The Western Wall (Kotel) is one of the main focal points for Simchat Torah in Jerusalem. The plaza fills through the evening of October 2, with Torah scrolls taken out of the Kotel's synagogue into the plaza area for hakafot. The crowd grows steadily from 8:00pm onward, with peak energy around 9:30–11:00pm.

**Logistics:**
- Enter via the Jaffa Gate or Dung Gate checkpoints; allow 10–15 minutes for security
- Dress modestly: covered shoulders and knees for both men and women; free kippot available at the entrance
- Separate sections for men and women, both with sightlines to the plaza celebrations
- No ticket or registration required — entry is open to all
- Photography is permitted in the outer plaza area; be respectful and avoid flash

The Western Wall plaza celebrations draw both deeply observant worshippers and secular Israeli families alongside international visitors — the full range of Jewish life in one plaza. The singing is in Hebrew, and the melodies are traditional enough that even first-time visitors quickly find themselves knowing the rhythm.

## Mea She'arim: the heart of the Jerusalem celebrations

For sheer intensity, nothing in Jerusalem matches Simchat Torah in **Mea She'arim**, the historic ultra-Orthodox neighbourhood ten minutes' walk from the centre of the New City. The narrow streets between the neighbourhood's synagogues fill with dancing processions; music pours from open doors; children wave small Torah-flag banners (degalim); families watch from balconies; the celebration continues until the early hours.

**For visitors, the Mea She'arim celebration is welcoming — with the right preparation:**

- **Dress strictly modestly.** For women: ankles, elbows, and hair covered. For men: long trousers, covered arms. This is non-negotiable in Mea She'arim at any time, and especially so on a holy day.
- **No cameras inside synagogue courtyards.** Photography on the street is acceptable if done discreetly and respectfully; never photograph someone who signals they don't want to be photographed.
- **No food, drink, smoking, or phone calls** (in the religious sense — using a phone to photograph is different from making calls, but exercise discretion).
- The main street (Mea She'arim Street) and the alley called "100 Gates" (Me'ah She'arim's inner courtyard area) are the primary viewing areas.
- A guided evening tour gives useful cultural context — see the [Jerusalem neighbourhood guide](/jerusalem-neighborhoods-guide) for orientation, or book a Mea She'arim walking tour via GetYourGuide.

**Timing:** The street celebrations are at their peak from about 9:00pm to midnight on Friday, October 2. Many congregations continue into the early hours of Saturday morning.

## Tel Aviv: Dizengoff Square and Carlebach synagogues

Tel Aviv's Simchat Torah has a character all its own — less intense than Jerusalem's Mea She'arim, more mixed in demographic, and organised around the city's famous public squares and open-minded religious communities.

### Dizengoff Square

The circular plaza at **Dizengoff Square** (Kikar Dizengoff) hosts outdoor hakafot that draw hundreds of families and young adults for dancing and singing. The celebration is inclusive and openly welcoming to visitors and non-Jews; the atmosphere is joyful and relaxed rather than strictly observant. Arrive from 8:30pm onwards on the evening of October 2.

### Carlebach-style synagogues

**Beit Tefila Yisraeli** and similar Carlebach-style congregations combine traditional Simchat Torah liturgy with the folk-melody repertoire of Rabbi Shlomo Carlebach — guitar, harmonium, and communal singing that draws large, mixed crowds. These congregations actively welcome visitors regardless of background. Check the synagogue's website or social channels closer to October 2 for confirmed locations and times.

### Practical Tel Aviv note

Unlike Jerusalem, Tel Aviv does not observe a complete Shabbat transport shutdown on Friday evenings — private services, the Dan bus company's night routes, and Tel Aviv's light rail operate. Getting around the city on Friday night is straightforward.

## Visitor etiquette across all sites

Simchat Torah is a genuine religious celebration — not a performance, and not primarily designed for tourists. These principles hold everywhere:

1. **Enter in a spirit of participation**, not spectatorship. Join the outer circles of dancing if invited or if it feels natural; observe with evident respect if you prefer.
2. **Dress appropriately.** Even at Dizengoff Square, modest dress shows respect and typically gets you a warmer welcome.
3. **Follow the lead of those around you.** If the people near you are not using phones, don't use yours. If a Torah scroll passes, step back to give it space.
4. **You do not need to be Jewish.** Simchat Torah is one of the most accessible Jewish holidays for visitors of any background — the joy is universal and the welcome is genuine for those who approach it respectfully.

## Connecting Simchat Torah to the rest of your trip

Simchat Torah falls at the very end of the Sukkot week, making it a natural extension of a High Holiday trip. If you have been in Israel for Sukkot, staying through Saturday, October 3 costs one extra night but transforms the trip's ending. The holiday concludes at nightfall on October 3; from Sunday, October 4, Israel returns to full operation — all transport, shops, restaurants, and tourist sites open normally.
