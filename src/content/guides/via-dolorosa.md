The Via Dolorosa is about 600 metres of ordinary Old City street, marked with fourteen stations, that runs from just inside Lions' Gate to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. Nine of the stations are outside, along a route that for much of its length is a working souk; the last five are inside the church. Walked without stopping it takes twenty minutes. Walked properly it takes ninety, and the hardest part is not the walking but finding stations Seven, Eight and Nine.

This guide gives each station, where it is and whether you can go inside; the Franciscan procession that walks it every Friday; how long to allow; whether to take a guide; what changes on Good Friday; and what to expect from the stretch that is a market rather than a pilgrimage route. Chapel hours and the procession time below were read on `seetheholyland.net` and `cicts.org` on **23 September 2026**.

## The fourteen stations, one by one

The route as walked today was fixed by the Franciscans in the eighteenth century and has been adjusted since; the numbering is what the plaques on the wall say, and that is what you follow.

**Station I — the condemnation.** In the courtyard of the **Umariyya School**, on the north side of the Via Dolorosa street just inside Lions' Gate. It is a working school, so the courtyard opens on school hours: `seetheholyland.net` lists Monday to Thursday and Saturday from 14:30 to 18:00, Friday 14:30 to 16:00. If it is closed, the route begins in the street outside, which is what most independent walkers do.

**Station II — the cross taken up.** Across the street, inside the **Franciscan Monastery of the Flagellation**. Two chapels share the courtyard: the **Chapel of the Flagellation**, with a crown-of-thorns dome, and the **Chapel of the Condemnation**, whose floor shows large flagstones long associated with the Roman pavement. Both open daily 08:00 to 12:00 and 14:00 to 17:00. The **Terra Sancta Museum** in the same compound is separately ticketed and keeps shorter hours. Just beyond, the **Ecce Homo Arch** spans the street; the rest of the same arch and the Roman-era pavement known as the **Lithostrotos** are inside the Sisters of Zion convent next door, a paid visit and the single best stop on the route for anyone interested in the archaeology.

**Station III — the first fall.** Where the Via Dolorosa street meets **al-Wad Street** and the route turns left. A small **Armenian Catholic chapel** marks it, with a relief above the door; you can usually go in.

**Station IV — the meeting with his mother.** A few metres further along al-Wad Street, at the Armenian Catholic church of Our Lady of the Spasm. There is a crypt below with a mosaic floor.

**Station V — Simon of Cyrene.** At the corner where the route turns right off al-Wad Street and begins to climb. A small **Franciscan chapel** stands on the corner; look for the worn hollow in the stone of the doorway that generations of pilgrims have touched.

**Station VI — Veronica.** Up the hill on the left, at the **Greek Catholic church of St Veronica**, part of a small convent of the Little Sisters of Jesus. Opening is irregular.

**Station VII — the second fall.** At the junction where the climbing lane crosses **Souk Khan al-Zeit**, the busiest market street in the Old City. Two small **Franciscan chapels** mark it. This is where most solo walkers lose the thread, because the route does not continue straight.

**Station VIII — the women of Jerusalem.** Cross the souk and continue up **Aqabat al-Khanqah** about thirty metres; a **cross carved into the wall** of a Greek Orthodox monastery marks it. There is nothing to enter; it is a stone in a wall and easy to walk past.

**Station IX — the third fall.** Back down to Souk Khan al-Zeit, north a short way, then **up a flight of about 28 steps** on your left and along a passage to the **Coptic Orthodox Patriarchate**. A **Roman column shaft** in the doorway is the marker. This detour is the part of the route almost nobody finds alone. From here the path continues past the Ethiopian monastery on the roof of the Holy Sepulchre's St Helena chapel and down into the church courtyard.

**Stations X to XIV — inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.** All five are within a few metres of each other:

- **X and XI** on the Calvary platform, in the Catholic Chapel of the Nailing to the Cross, up the steep stairs on your right as you enter the church.
- **XII** in the Greek Orthodox Chapel of the Crucifixion beside it.
- **XIII** at the Catholic altar of Our Lady of Sorrows between the two, with the Stone of Anointing on the floor below at the entrance.
- **XIV** the **Aedicule** in the Rotunda, the shrine over the Tomb, and the only part of the route with a queue.

Our [Church of the Holy Sepulchre guide](/church-of-the-holy-sepulchre) covers those five in detail, along with opening hours, the queue for the Tomb and the Mass schedule.

## The Franciscan procession on Fridays

Every Friday afternoon the Franciscans lead a public procession along the whole route, and it is the best thing on this page.

**Where and when.** It forms in the courtyard of the **Umariyya School**, just inside Lions' Gate, and sets off at **16:00, or 15:00 from November to March** (`seetheholyland.net`, read 23 September 2026). Allow roughly an hour and a half to reach the Holy Sepulchre, moving slowly and stopping at each station for a reading and a prayer.

**Who can join.** Anyone. It is free, nothing is booked, and there is no registration. Pilgrim groups, parish groups and individual visitors all fall in behind the friars, and the readings are given in several languages as the procession moves. You can also simply stand at a corner and let it pass.

**Why it is worth planning a Friday around.** Walking the route alone on a Tuesday, you are threading past stalls selling scarves and phone cases while looking for a plaque. In the procession the street reorganises itself around the walk for ninety minutes: shopkeepers step back, the crowd follows, and the route reads as a single continuous thing rather than fourteen disconnected marks. It is also the one time the whole route is navigated for you.

**Two cautions.** Friday is the Muslim day of congregational prayer and the Jewish approach to Shabbat, so the Old City is at its busiest and the Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif is closed to non-Muslim visitors all day. And the procession moves at the pace of a crowd on uneven stone; if stairs are difficult, join for the flatter first half and meet the group at the church.

## How long it takes, and how to fit it into a day

| Way of walking it | Time to allow |
| --- | --- |
| Straight through, no stops | About 20 minutes |
| Independently, stopping at each station | 90 minutes |
| With the Friday procession | About 90 minutes, fixed start |
| With a guide, including the Lithostrotos and the Coptic patriarchate | Half a day |
| Add the Church of the Holy Sepulchre | 60 to 90 minutes, plus whatever the Tomb queue is |

**The classic Jerusalem morning** puts it in a line that works geographically rather than as a list of sights: taxi to the top of the **Mount of Olives** at first light, walk down past the lookout, Dominus Flevit and **Gethsemane** to **Lions' Gate**, pick up the **Via Dolorosa** at the First Station, and arrive at the **Holy Sepulchre** by mid-morning. That is three to four hours and it is the single best half-day in the city. The one flaw is that it puts you at the church after the coaches; if entering the Tomb matters more than the sequence, do the Holy Sepulchre at opening and walk the Via Dolorosa backwards afterwards, which is easier than it sounds because the stations are numbered.

**Where to pick it up if you are short of time.** Coming from Jaffa Gate or the light rail, join at the Seventh Station where the route crosses Souk Khan al-Zeit; you will do the last third and the church in an hour.

## Alone or with a guide

**Walk it alone if** you want an hour to yourself, you are comfortable reading a map in a crowded market, and you are content to see the stations rather than go inside. The plaques are there, the route is public and free, and an offline map on your phone solves most of it.

**Take a guide if** you want the archaeology. Three things on this route are effectively invisible without one: the **Lithostrotos** pavement and the Struthion Pool under the Sisters of Zion convent, the fact that the medieval and modern street levels sit several metres above the Roman one, and the **Coptic patriarchate** at the Ninth Station, which is up an unmarked staircase. A guide also gets you through stations Seven to Nine without the three wrong turns everyone makes. **The honest middle option** is the Friday procession, which is free, requires no booking and removes the navigation problem entirely, and then coming back another morning to go inside the chapels at your own pace.

## Good Friday and Holy Week

Good Friday is the busiest day of the year on the Via Dolorosa, twice over in years when the two Easters fall apart. In **2027, Western Good Friday is 26 March and Orthodox Good Friday is 30 April** — five weeks apart, the maximum possible gap, so Jerusalem gets two separate peak weeks rather than one.

What to expect on either of those days: the Franciscan procession runs but with crowds in the thousands rather than the dozens; individual parish, national and religious-order groups walk the stations in their own languages from early morning until evening; some Old City streets are closed or made one-way by police; the souk is impassable at the junctions; and the Holy Sepulchre is full all day, with access to the Aedicule restricted or suspended for ceremonies. Old City accommodation is booked out months ahead for both weeks.

If you are in Jerusalem for Holy Week and not attached to a group, the practical advice is to walk the route on the **Tuesday or Wednesday** instead, when it is busy but passable, and to watch Friday rather than try to move through it. ## The stretch that is a souk

From roughly the Third Station to the Ninth, the Via Dolorosa is a shopping street. Along al-Wad Street and across Souk Khan al-Zeit you walk past spice sacks, bakeries pulling out trays of ka'ak, juice presses, fabric stalls and racks of souvenirs, with delivery trolleys pushing through the middle. Visitors arrive expecting a quiet processional way and find a market, and that mismatch is the single most common disappointment reported about the route.

It is worth reframing before you go: this has been a commercial street for as long as it has been a pilgrimage route, and the two have coexisted for centuries. Practically:

- **Go early.** Before 09:00 the souk is opening rather than trading and the route is genuinely quiet.
- **Watch your bag.** Crowded, narrow and full of stops and starts; a zipped bag worn in front is the sensible default here as in any market.
- **Prices are negotiable** in the souvenir stalls and fixed in the food shops. Nobody is obliged to haggle.
- **Do not expect quiet at the stations.** Stations Seven and Eight in particular are marks on walls in the middle of trade, and groups praying at them do so with the market going on around.
- **Friday afternoon** is the exception, because the procession slows the street down.

## Getting there and what is nearby

The First Station is a five-minute walk inside **Lions' Gate**, on the east side of the Old City, which is where the Mount of Olives descent delivers you. From West Jerusalem, the simplest approach is the light rail to City Hall, in through **Jaffa Gate**, and either walk the route backwards from the Holy Sepulchre or cross the Old City to Lions' Gate first, about twenty minutes through the Muslim Quarter.

**Nearby and worth adding.** St Anne's Church, just inside Lions' Gate, is a spare Crusader building with famous acoustics and the Pools of Bethesda excavation in its garden. The **Austrian Hospice** rooftop, at the corner of al-Wad Street and the Via Dolorosa, is a few shekels for coffee and the best free-standing view over the Old City's roofs, which is also the clearest way to understand how the route sits in the city. The **Western Wall** is ten minutes down al-Wad Street.
