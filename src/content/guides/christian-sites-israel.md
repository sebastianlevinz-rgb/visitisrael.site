Israel and the Palestinian territories hold the sites of the Christian gospels in an area you can cross in three hours, and almost every organised itinerary visits the same short list. This page is the map of that list: which sites are in which region, which ones appear in nearly every itinerary and which are specific to one tradition, how the calendar shapes the year, what a single guided day costs against a full package, what you can do on your own, and one thing nobody in this market says out loud about language.

Everything here is logistics: where things are, when they open, how a visit works and what an itinerary includes. Figures and schedules were read on **23 September 2026** and are attributed where they came from a published page. We do not print exact prices, because they change and because the live figure belongs on the operator's booking page, not on ours.

## The sites, region by region

The ordering below comes from counting how many of **twelve published Holy Land itineraries** listed each site, read on 23 September 2026. A site not listed does not mean the tour skips it; several operators summarise rather than itemise. The count is useful for ranking, not as a census.

### Jerusalem and the Mount of Olives

- **Church of the Holy Sepulchre** — in ten of the twelve itineraries. Free, open before dawn to evening, every day of the year, with a queue for the Tomb from mid-morning. The [full guide](/church-of-the-holy-sepulchre) has the hours, the Mass schedule and the walk in from each gate.
- **Via Dolorosa** — ten of twelve. Fourteen stations from Lions' Gate to the Holy Sepulchre, walkable free at any hour, with a Franciscan procession every Friday afternoon. See the [Via Dolorosa guide](/via-dolorosa).
- **Mount of Olives** — ten of twelve. The lookout, the Pater Noster church, Dominus Flevit, St Mary Magdalene and the descent to the bottom.
- **Gethsemane** — eight of twelve. The garden with its ancient olive trees and the Church of All Nations beside it, at the foot of the Mount of Olives descent.
- **Mount Zion and the Cenacle** — six of twelve, and rarely as a main stop. The Cenacle and the Dormition Abbey are a five-minute walk outside Zion Gate.
- **Ein Karem** — four of twelve, and essentially only on Catholic itineraries. Two churches in a green valley below Yad Vashem, and the best lunch stop in West Jerusalem.
- **Garden Tomb** — two of twelve, and only on the Protestant ones. A walled garden near Damascus Gate; see the tradition section below.

Our [Jerusalem guide](/jerusalem) puts all of these into a two- or three-day plan with the non-Christian sites of the city.

### Bethlehem and the south

- **Church of the Nativity, Bethlehem** — eleven of twelve itineraries, and the one stop every operator flags for passports. It sits on Manger Square with the Grotto of the Nativity beneath the altar, St Catherine's Church next door and the Milk Grotto a short walk away.
- **Jericho** — seven of twelve, usually paired with the Jordan River as a full day.
- **Masada and the Dead Sea** — eight of twelve, generally as a landscape or free day rather than a Christian stop.

**How Bethlehem actually works**, because most pages either skip it or reassure you about it. Bethlehem is about ten kilometres south of the Old City, in the West Bank under Palestinian Authority administration, and you pass through a checkpoint at the separation barrier. You need your **physical passport**, not a copy. **Israeli rental cars are generally not insured** for Palestinian Authority-administered areas, so driving your own hire car in is not an option. On a guided day trip the standard procedure is a **change of vehicle and guide at the crossing**: an Israeli-licensed guide hands over to a Palestinian guide and a local vehicle for the Bethlehem portion, then back again. Tourists are normally processed in minutes on the return and receive no stamp. Some operators state that holders of Israeli passports cannot join. Our [Bethlehem day trip guide](/jerusalem-bethlehem-day-trip) has the full procedure, the bus and taxi options, and what the tour formats include.

### Nazareth and the Galilee

- **Nazareth and the Basilica of the Annunciation** — twelve of twelve. The only site in the whole count that appears in every single itinerary.
- **Sea of Galilee** — twelve of twelve, almost always with a wooden-boat crossing.
- **Capernaum** — nine of twelve. The synagogue, the excavated village and the modern church over the traditional house of Peter.
- **Jordan River, at Yardenit or Qasr el Yahud** — nine of twelve, though nine catalogues say only "Jordan River" without saying which site.
- **Cana** — eight of twelve, and predominantly on Catholic itineraries.
- **Mount Tabor** — eight of twelve, also predominantly Catholic. The last stretch is a switchback road that large coaches cannot take, so operators transfer to minibuses or taxis.
- **Mount of Beatitudes** — five of twelve.
- **Tabgha** — three of twelve, fewer than its reputation suggests.

The [Galilee region guide](/galilee) covers the lake and its shore, and the [Nazareth and Sea of Galilee day trip](/nazareth-sea-of-galilee-day-trip) is the single-day version of this whole list from Tel Aviv or Jerusalem.

### One site that is on nobody's itinerary

**Emmaus** appeared in none of the twelve itineraries, and in none of the listings on the three big booking marketplaces either. There are several candidate locations and no consensus, which is presumably why the commercial itineraries leave it out. If it matters to you, it is a self-drive or private-guide addition, not something you will find on a catalogue.

## The calendar: when to go, and the year that breaks the usual advice

Every operator recommends travelling in May, after the Holy Week crush and before the summer heat. **In 2027 that advice is wrong**, and as far as we can tell nobody selling Holy Land trips has adjusted it.

The reason is that the two Easters fall **35 days apart in 2027, the maximum possible gap**. Western Easter is Sunday 28 March, with Palm Sunday on 21 March and Good Friday on 26 March. Orthodox Pascha is Sunday 2 May, with the Holy Fire ceremony at the Holy Sepulchre on Holy Saturday, 1 May. So Jerusalem gets **two separate pilgrim peaks in the same spring**, and the first week of May is the second of them, not the quiet window.

Other dates that change where you want to be:

- **Bethlehem has three Christmases**: 24 to 25 December, 6 to 7 January and 18 to 19 January. The Latin Patriarch's procession into Manger Square is on the afternoon of 24 December and the Midnight Mass in St Catherine's is ticketed through the Latin Patriarchate and sells out.
- **The Annunciation is displaced in 2027.** 25 March 2027 falls in Holy Week, so in the Roman calendar the solemnity moves to **Monday 5 April 2027**. For anyone travelling to Nazareth for that feast, it is the difference between arriving and missing it. Verified against the USCCB's 2027 liturgical calendar, read 23 September 2026.
- **Shabbat** stops the trains and buses from Friday afternoon to Saturday night across the country, while the Old City's Christian and Muslim Quarters and the Holy Sepulchre stay open. Saturday is a good day for the Old City and for Bethlehem, and a bad one for anything needing Israeli public transport.

The [Shabbat and holidays guide](/shabbat-and-holidays-israel) carries the full 2026 to 2027 calendar across all three religious calendars, with what closes on each date.

**Where the operators actually run their departures**, which is the most useful signal in the market: September to November and February to March. They sell outside the peaks because in the peaks they cannot get rooms. If your dates are flexible, follow them.

## Differences between traditions, treated as visiting logistics

Itineraries differ in ways that change your day, and the differences are easier to plan around when they are described as what they are: different stops, different bookings, different opening hours. Nothing below is a judgement about any tradition, and none of it is doctrine.

**Two sites, two ways of visiting.** The **Church of the Holy Sepulchre** and the **Garden Tomb** are fifteen minutes apart on foot and they work completely differently as visits. The Holy Sepulchre is free, opens before dawn and closes in the evening every day of the year, is shared by several communities whose liturgies run through the day, and has a queue for the Tomb that reaches about an hour by mid-morning. The Garden Tomb, near Damascus Gate, is a walled garden with free entry, limited hours and closed days, and it takes bookings from church groups that want to hold a communion service on site. **We are not publishing its hours**: the Garden Tomb's own domain gave us two contradictory schedules on 23 September 2026 — one saying 09:00 to 13:00 with Fridays and Sundays closed, another saying Monday to Saturday in two blocks with Sunday closed — and we could not resolve which is current. **Check gardentomb.com before you go.** Catholic and Orthodox itineraries end the Jerusalem day at the Holy Sepulchre; Protestant and evangelical ones usually end it at the Garden Tomb. Travellers who want both should simply do both; nothing stops you.

**Two baptism sites on the same river.** **Yardenit** sits where the Jordan leaves the Sea of Galilee, is privately run, has free entry with robes to rent or buy, and slots naturally into a Galilee day. **Qasr el Yahud** is far to the south near Jericho, at the traditional site, run by the Israel Nature and Parks Authority with free entry, and slots into a Jericho or Dead Sea day; the Nature and Parks Authority lists it open Sunday to Thursday and Saturday, roughly 08:00 to 17:00 in summer and to 16:00 in winter, with last entry an hour before closing. Catholic itineraries tend toward Qasr el Yahud for a renewal of baptismal promises; evangelical ones tend to programme a full immersion service as a scheduled item. **Nine of the twelve itineraries we read simply said "Jordan River"** without naming the site, so if it matters to you, ask before you book: the two are ninety minutes apart.

**Daily Mass is an itinerary feature, not a default.** Some operators state in writing that a chaplain travels with the group and a Eucharist is celebrated daily; others never mention a service at all; at least one promises a chaplain only if the group reaches twenty people. This is worth checking because it determines the shape of the day: a group with a booked Mass at a sanctuary has a fixed anchor in its morning. Mass slots in the Franciscan sanctuaries are allocated by the **Franciscan Pilgrims Office** at Jaffa Gate, and the Custody publishes the Holy Sepulchre schedule itself, with the important note that the early-morning slots on Calvary only run **if a group has booked one**. The [Holy Sepulchre guide](/church-of-the-holy-sepulchre) has the full schedule, the languages and the office's contact details.

**Stops that mark one itinerary or another.** Cana, Mount Tabor, Ein Karem and the Cenacle appear mostly on Catholic itineraries. Megiddo, Caesarea Philippi, Dan, Korazim, Mount Arbel, the City of David and Masada appear mostly on Protestant and evangelical ones. If a catalogue does not say which tradition it is built for, the stop list tells you.

## One day, several days, or a package

**A single guided day** is the entry point and the most flexible option. The three that matter are Jerusalem and Bethlehem in one day, Nazareth and the Sea of Galilee in one day, and an Old City walk ending at the Holy Sepulchre. Read on 23 September 2026, Israeli operators' own Christian day tours ran from roughly **US$75 to US$125 per person** for a full day, with private full-day guiding priced per vehicle rather than per person and worth comparing for a family or a group of four or more. The same tours are resold on the big marketplaces, generally at a higher headline price than booking direct with the operator running them.

**Two or three days on your own** covers the core: Jerusalem and the Old City on foot, a half-day to Bethlehem, and a full guided or self-drive day in the Galilee. That is the efficient version for an independent traveller and it is what most readers of this site will actually do.

**A seven- to twelve-day package** is what the pilgrimage specialists sell, and nine or ten days is the most common length. They add Cana, Mount Tabor, Jericho, the Jordan River, Masada and the Dead Sea, include half or full board, and move at the pace of a coach group. Read on 23 September 2026, Israeli operators listed these from roughly **US$1,450 to US$2,500 per person, land only**; European and Latin American pilgrimage agencies published figures from about **1,300 to 5,700 euros** or **US$1,400 to US$6,300** for comparable lengths.

**The comparison trap, and it is a real one.** Those figures are not comparable with each other, and almost nobody says so. Some include the international flight from Madrid, Mexico City or New York; some start at Ben Gurion Airport and include nothing before it; some quote land-only and add the airfare separately. Tips are included by some operators and charged separately by others, in one published case at over a hundred US dollars per person for the trip. **Before comparing two prices, normalise them**: flight in or out, board basis, tips, single supplement, and airport-transfer arrangements. ## On your own or with a guide

**Do it independently if** your focus is Jerusalem. The Old City is a square kilometre, everything Christian in it is free, and you can walk between the Holy Sepulchre, the Via Dolorosa, the Mount of Olives and Mount Zion in a day and a half. Bethlehem is straightforward too: the Arab buses from opposite Damascus Gate take about forty minutes, you cross on foot and take a local taxi the last few minutes.

**Take a guide for the Galilee.** Capernaum, Tabgha, the Mount of Beatitudes, Magdala and the Jordan are spread around a lake with poor public transport between them, and a day tour or a rental car is the only practical way to link them. The same applies to Mount Tabor, where the final ascent needs a small vehicle.

**Take a guide in the Old City if you want the archaeology explained.** Nothing inside the Holy Sepulchre is labelled and the Via Dolorosa's stations seven to nine are genuinely hard to find. A licensed guide also names their own credential: the Israeli guiding licence, the Commissariat of the Holy Land, a diocesan accreditation. Asking which one is a fair question and the answer distinguishes operators quickly. Our [Jerusalem tours compared](/jerusalem-tours-compared) guide covers the formats and how to choose.

## Language: what is available and what is not

This is the part of the market that is not advertised honestly, so here it is plainly, as read on **23 September 2026**.

**Single days are widely available in Spanish, and also in German and French.** Several Israeli operators guide their Jerusalem full-day, Bethlehem, Nazareth and Galilee day tours in those languages, and the large marketplaces list Spanish-language departures for the main Jerusalem and Bethlehem products. If you want one guided day in Spanish, you will find it.

**Long packages in Spanish are a different matter.** Among the seven- to twelve-day Christian Holy Land packages sold by Israeli operators and by the marketplaces, **we did not find one that stated Spanish-language guiding**; the ones we could read were in English. The Spanish-speaking market for long pilgrimages is served instead by pilgrimage agencies in Spain, Mexico, Colombia and elsewhere, which sell their own departures with their own Spanish-speaking guides and, in several cases, an accompanying priest. That is a genuinely different product bought through a different channel, and if a full Spanish-language pilgrimage is what you want, that is where to look rather than on an international booking site.

**Portuguese is thinner still.** We found no tour offered in Portuguese on any of the six platforms and operator catalogues we checked.

**One warning about buying in your own language.** A marketplace selling in Spanish does not necessarily operate in Spanish. At least one large Spanish-language platform lists a Bethlehem excursion from Jerusalem that carries a low user score and a stated mismatch between the advertised language and the language the guide actually works in, according to its own listing and reviews. Whatever platform you use, **read the language field on the specific departure**, not the language of the website.
