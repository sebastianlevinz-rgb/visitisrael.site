**Shavuot** is the Jewish harvest festival and the commemoration of the giving of the Torah at Mount Sinai. Celebrated fifty days after Passover, it is Israel's most food-scented holiday — dairy spreads overflow from market stalls for weeks — and home to one of the most extraordinary pre-dawn gatherings in the Jerusalem calendar: thousands of people who have spent the night in Torah study converging on the Western Wall plaza at sunrise for morning prayers.

For visitors, Shavuot offers two rare experiences that require no religious background, only a willingness to be awake at 4 am and to dress modestly: the Tikkun Leil atmosphere in the Old City, where synagogues and study halls remain open and lit through the night, and the dawn prayer at the [Western Wall](/western-wall-guide), where the plaza fills with an extraordinary crowd — exhausted, joyful, and deeply present — as the Jerusalem sky turns light.

---

## Shavuot 2027 dates

In Israel, Shavuot is observed for **one day** (two days in the Diaspora). The 2027 holiday shares its distinctive calendar pattern with 2026: Shavuot falls on a Thursday evening and rolls directly into Shabbat, producing a three-day consecutive closure window.

| Period | 2027 | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Shavuot begins | Evening of Thursday 10 June | Synagogues begin Tikkun Leil study from nightfall |
| Shavuot day | Friday, 11 June | Full Yom Tov closures; Western Wall dawn service; dairy markets and restaurant menus |
| Shabbat begins | Friday evening, 11 June | Shavuot ends and Shabbat immediately follows |
| Three-day window ends | Saturday night, 12 June | Restaurants and transport resume |

The back-to-back Shavuot + Shabbat run in 2027 is the same calendar coincidence that occurred in 2026. Stock up on food and supplies before Thursday evening, and arrange all transport in advance — two consecutive days of closures can catch unprepared visitors short.

## The Western Wall dawn walk

The single most memorable Shavuot experience for visitors is the **pre-dawn convergence at the Western Wall**. From about 2 am onward on Shavuot night, individuals and groups who have been studying Torah through the night make their way from synagogues across the Jewish Quarter and from all over the city down to the Kotel plaza. By 4–4:30 am the plaza holds a striking crowd — all ages, all backgrounds, many in tallit (prayer shawls) or white shirts, often carrying children on their shoulders or leaning quietly against the Wall. The Shacharit morning prayer service begins as dawn breaks.

**Practical guide:**

- **Arrive 3:30–4:30 am** to find a spot before the densest crowds gather.
- Entry is free and open to all visitors regardless of religion or background.
- **Dress modestly**: covered shoulders and knees for both men and women; kippot are available free at the entrance for men; headscarves for women.
- **Getting there overnight**: Old City buses run through the night on Shavuot in Jerusalem; taxis (Gett, Yango) operate as usual. Walking from the Jewish Quarter is 10 minutes; from the Old City's Jaffa Gate, allow 15 minutes.
- **Temperature**: Jerusalem in June at 4 am is around 18–20°C — bring a light jacket.
- **Photography etiquette**: many worshippers prefer not to be photographed during prayer; be aware and respectful.

## Tikkun Leil Shavuot: the night of study

The Tikkun is an all-night Torah study session rooted in Kabbalistic tradition from 16th-century Safed — the idea being that the Jewish people must "prepare" through learning to receive the Torah as they did at Sinai. In practice, every synagogue in Jerusalem holds a Tikkun from nightfall; the Old City's Jewish Quarter has dozens within walking distance of each other.

For visitors, the most atmospheric approach is simply to **walk through the Jewish Quarter between midnight and 2 am** on Shavuot night. Study halls and synagogues glow with light; open windows carry the sound of prayer and Talmudic debate into the ancient stone alleyways. Some yeshivot and synagogues in Mea She'arim are open to respectful visitors on the street; the Aish HaTorah centre on the Western Wall promenade typically runs English-language programming.

In **Tel Aviv**, the Tikkun takes a more secular form: cultural venues, rooftops, and university spaces host lectures, music, and open-learning events on Shavuot night. Check secrettelaviv.com and the Tel Aviv municipality events calendar (tlv.gov.il) for current-year programming.

## Dairy food traditions

The custom of eating dairy on Shavuot transforms Israeli markets and restaurants for the week surrounding the holiday. At [Mahane Yehuda market](/mahane-yehuda-market-guide) in Jerusalem and the Carmel Market in Tel Aviv, artisan cheese stalls expand significantly: labneh in olive oil, Bulgarian white cheese, goat's cheese logs, and fresh ricotta crowd the dairy vendors. The iconic Shavuot desserts are **cheesecake** (עוגת גבינה — an Israeli style that is lighter and tangier than New York cheesecake) and **blintzes** (cheese-filled crêpes with fruit or sour cream). Bakeries across both cities run Shavuot dairy specials from early June.

The best time to visit the markets for the Shavuot food experience is the **day before the holiday** — stalls are fully stocked and the pre-holiday energy is at its peak. During the holiday day itself, Mahane Yehuda and most markets in Jewish neighbourhoods are closed.

## The three-day closure: how to prepare

In 2027, Shavuot falls on Friday. When Shavuot ends at nightfall on Friday, Shabbat begins immediately — producing two consecutive days of closures (Friday Shavuot + Saturday Shabbat) plus the Thursday evening preceding. This three-day window requires the same preparation as any extended Shabbat/holiday overlap:

- **Stock food and supplies by Thursday afternoon** before Shavuot begins.
- **Book transport in advance** — taxis and apps (Gett, Yango) continue operating, but intercity buses suspend.
- **Arab-owned businesses** in East Jerusalem, Nazareth, Akko, Jaffa, and Haifa's Arab neighbourhoods are unaffected and generally remain open throughout.
- **Tourist hotels** maintain full services, including meals, reception, and amenities, on both days.

For the full weekly Shabbat logistics — what shuts down and what stays open — see the guide to [what's open on Shabbat in Israel](/whats-open-on-shabbat).

## Jerusalem vs Tel Aviv for Shavuot

**Jerusalem** is the natural heart of Shavuot. The Western Wall dawn walk, the Tikkun atmosphere in the Old City, and the proximity of synagogues and study halls make it the most immersive experience. The [1-day Jerusalem itinerary](/1-day-jerusalem-itinerary) gives a useful framework for the surrounding sightseeing.

**Tel Aviv** offers a more secular Shavuot — cultural Tikkun events, dairy-themed restaurant menus across Neve Tzedek and Rothschild, and the beaches open and inviting in June's warm weather. For visitors who want Shavuot atmosphere without the Old City intensity, Tel Aviv is a lighter, more accessible choice.

**Both cities** run dairy food markets the week before the holiday. Combining the two — a Thursday in Tel Aviv's Carmel Market + overnight bus or taxi to Jerusalem for the Tikkun and dawn walk — is feasible for visitors willing to travel on Shavuot eve.
