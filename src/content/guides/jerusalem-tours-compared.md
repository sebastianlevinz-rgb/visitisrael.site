**The short answer:** if it is your first visit, take a guided half-day walking tour of the Old City and explore the rest on your own. If you have one day and are based in Tel Aviv, take a full-day Jerusalem tour with transport. Christian pilgrims should add Bethlehem or book a private guide who knows the churches; history-minded visitors should add the Western Wall Tunnels and the City of David; families do best with a private guide who can set the pace. Below, every type of Jerusalem tour compared on duration, starting point, group size, what is included and what it roughly costs, plus the days to avoid and how to do the city without a tour at all. For the sights themselves, pair this with our [Jerusalem region guide](/jerusalem).

## Which Jerusalem tour is right for you?

Jerusalem has more guided-tour options than anywhere else in Israel, and they are not interchangeable. A three-hour Old City walk, a ten-hour coach day from Tel Aviv and a private pilgrimage itinerary are different trips that happen to share a city. The quickest way to narrow it down is by profile.

- **First time, based in Jerusalem, two or more days:** a half-day Old City walking tour on your first morning, then self-guided time. Add a Western Wall Tunnels slot or the City of David on day two.
- **First time, based in Tel Aviv, one day for Jerusalem:** a full-day tour with pickup. The train is easy, but a tour saves you the navigation and packs in the Mount of Olives, which is awkward to reach alone.
- **Christian pilgrim:** the Jerusalem and Bethlehem combination, or a private guide for a Via Dolorosa walk at a pace that allows prayer. Check whether the tour is denominational (some are led by Catholic or Evangelical guides and include mass or devotions).
- **Jewish heritage:** the Western Wall Tunnels, the City of David and the Jewish Quarter's Herodian-era sites; Yad Vashem is a half day on its own.
- **Interested in Islamic Jerusalem:** the Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif has limited visiting hours for non-Muslims (see below); a few walking tours cover the Muslim Quarter's markets and Mamluk architecture.
- **History and archaeology:** City of David plus the Western Wall Tunnels; the Israel Museum on your own.
- **Family with children under 12:** a private guide, or the City of David's water tunnel, which children love. Standard group walks are long on standing and listening.
- **Only a few hours:** a two-to-three-hour Old City highlights walk from Jaffa Gate, or an evening Mahane Yehuda food walk.

## Types of Jerusalem tours, explained

### Old City walking tours (half day)

The default first-timer choice. A licensed guide meets you at Jaffa Gate and walks the four quarters in three to four hours: the Armenian Quarter, the Jewish Quarter and the Western Wall plaza, the Muslim Quarter's markets, the Via Dolorosa and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. The value is in the layering. The same street is three different stories depending on who is telling it, and a good guide holds all three at once. Group sizes on platform-sold walks run from about eight to twenty-five; the price usually covers the guide only, with no entrance fees (the main sites in the Old City are free anyway) and no food. Most run daily except Saturday, with a Friday start early enough to be done before the Muslim Quarter fills for noon prayers.

### Full-day Jerusalem tours (with or without transport from Tel Aviv)

Seven to ten hours. The Tel Aviv versions add roughly an hour each way by coach and typically start with the Mount of Olives panorama, then the Old City, then Mount Zion (King David's Tomb and the Room of the Last Supper) or a drive past the Knesset and the Israel Museum. Some end with a stop at Yad Vashem. Groups are larger, often thirty to fifty on a coach, and the day is tight: expect about an hour at the Holy Sepulchre and Western Wall combined. These are the right tour for a single day, not the best tour of Jerusalem. The same full-day itinerary also departs from Jerusalem hotels, without the coach transfer, and is cheaper. Note that Yad Vashem does not admit children under ten to its main museum, so family-oriented tours substitute another stop.

### Christian pilgrimage tours

These follow the Gospel geography: Bethany and the Mount of Olives (Pater Noster, Dominus Flevit, Gethsemane), the Via Dolorosa with the Stations of the Cross, the Holy Sepulchre, the Garden Tomb (the alternative site favoured by many Protestant groups), Mount Zion and often Ein Karem or Bethlehem. Most multi-day Holy Land packages, covered in our [Israel tour packages guide](/israel-tour-packages), give Jerusalem two or three days. As a day tour, look for guides who state their tradition and allow time for devotion; the platform listings that say "In the Footsteps of Jesus" are usually the full-day version for mixed groups.

### Jewish heritage tours

The Western Wall Tunnels, the Jewish Quarter's Cardo and Herodian Quarter, the Davidson Center's Southern Wall excavations and the City of David together make a long day underground and above it. Some operators add Mount Herzl and the Machane Yehuda market for the modern story; Friday-afternoon tours end at the Western Wall as Shabbat comes in, which is a scene worth planning around even if you are not Jewish.

### City of David and the Western Wall Tunnels

Two separate sites, two separate bookings, often paired. The **City of David** national park sits just outside Dung Gate. Its centrepiece is Hezekiah's Tunnel, a 2,700-year-old water channel you wade through in knee- to thigh-deep water with a torch for about 40 minutes; there is a dry Canaanite tunnel alternative for anyone who prefers not to get wet. Guided tours also take in the Pilgrimage Road excavation. Bring water shoes and a change of trousers for the wet route. The **Western Wall Tunnels**, entered from the northern end of the Western Wall plaza, follow the buried continuation of the wall along its Herodian courses, past the largest ashlars in the complex and along a Second Temple-period street. Entry is by guided tour only, in timed slots of about 75 to 90 minutes, run by the Western Wall Heritage Foundation and resold by platforms; slots sell out weeks ahead in spring and autumn. Both sites close on Saturday and Jewish holidays.

### Yad Vashem

The World Holocaust Remembrance Center on Mount Herzl. Admission is free, but book a time slot online in advance; children under ten are not admitted to the Holocaust History Museum. Guided tours exist but most visitors self-guide with the audio guide over two to three hours. It closes on Saturdays and Jewish holidays and early on Fridays. Reach it by light rail (Mount Herzl stop) and a short shuttle or walk. If a full-day city tour includes it as a one-hour stop, know that an hour is not enough.

### Mahane Yehuda market tours (day and night)

The shuk is two markets. By day, from Sunday to Friday early afternoon, it is produce, spices, halva, bakeries and the best street food in Jerusalem; food tours of about two hours run in the morning and at lunchtime and include six to ten tastings. After dark, from Saturday night to Thursday, the shutters come down and the same alleys turn into bars. Night tours are shorter and lighter on food. Neither runs on Friday night or Saturday. Compare with our [Tel Aviv food tours](/tel-aviv-food-tours) if you are doing both cities.

### Jerusalem and Bethlehem combination tours

The Old City in the morning and Bethlehem, ten kilometres south in the West Bank, in the afternoon: the Church of the Nativity, the Grotto, the Milk Grotto and often Shepherds' Field. Because Israeli rental cars are not insured for Palestinian Authority areas, and because Israeli-licensed guides generally hand over to a licensed Palestinian guide at the checkpoint, a tour is by far the simplest way to combine the two. Bring your passport. Bethlehem-only half-day tours also run from Jerusalem. Our [Jerusalem and Bethlehem day trip guide](/jerusalem-bethlehem-day-trip) has the full logistics and what the checkpoint involves.

### Private tours

A licensed guide, with or without a driver, for a half or full day. You set the sites, the pace and the emphasis, whether that is a denominational route, an archaeological one or a child-friendly one. Prices are per group, so for a family of four or two couples the per-person cost approaches that of a coach tour while the experience is entirely different. Israeli tour guides are state-licensed after a two-year course; ask for the licence number, and for a guide whose specialism matches your interest.

## How to compare Jerusalem tours before booking

Read the listing, not the title. The same six details decide whether a tour fits.

| Full-day Jerusalem (from Tel Aviv) | 9–10 h | Hotel pickup, Tel Aviv | Coach | Guide, transport; no entries or lunch | Days ahead |
| Full-day Jerusalem (from Jerusalem) | 7–8 h | Jerusalem hotels or a meeting point | Coach or minibus | Guide, transport within the city | Days ahead |
| Jerusalem & Bethlehem combo | 8–9 h | Jerusalem or Tel Aviv | Coach or minibus | Guide(s), transport, checkpoint handled | Days ahead |
| Western Wall Tunnels | 1¼–1½ h | Western Wall plaza | Up to about 30 | Guide and entry | Weeks ahead in peak season |
| City of David | 2–3 h | Dung Gate | Small–medium | Guide and entry | Days ahead |
| Yad Vashem | 2–3 h self-guided | Mount Herzl | Individual | Free entry; audio guide extra | Timed slot required |

| Private guide | Half or full day | Your hotel | Your party | Guide; driver optional | 1–2 weeks ahead in season |

**Duration.** A listed "7 hours" from Tel Aviv includes two hours on the coach. A "3-hour" Old City walk usually runs long.

**Starting point.** Jaffa Gate is the standard meeting point for walks; Dung Gate for the City of David; the Western Wall plaza for the Tunnels. Tel Aviv pickups begin early, often before 7 am, and collect from several hotels, so the first hour is spent in the city.

**Language.** English tours run daily. Spanish, French, German, Italian, Russian and Portuguese depart less often, sometimes only on set weekdays; filter by language on the platform rather than assuming.

**Group size.** Listings say "small group" for anything under about fifteen. Coach tours can reach fifty. In the Holy Sepulchre a group of fifty moves slowly and hears little.

**What is included.** Most Jerusalem tours include the guide and, where relevant, transport. Entrance fees to the City of David, the Tunnels and Mount Zion sites are often extra and paid on the day; lunch is almost never included and is taken in the Old City at your own cost. Headsets are a plus on large tours.

**Cancellation.** The big platforms default to free cancellation up to 24 hours before; some operators require 48 or 72 hours. Given that closures and security restrictions can shift schedules, book only tours with free cancellation, and read our [safety guide](/is-israel-safe) before you go.

## Days and times to avoid

**Shabbat.** From Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall, the Jewish Quarter shops, the Western Wall Tunnels, the City of David, Yad Vashem and the Israel Museum are closed, and public transport in Jerusalem stops (the light rail and buses resume Saturday night). The Western Wall itself is open and busiest, but photography there is not permitted on Shabbat. The Christian and Muslim Quarters and the Holy Sepulchre are open, and Saturday is a reasonable day for a Christian-focused walk or for the Mount of Olives. Almost no scheduled group tours run on Saturday; the exception is a handful of private guides and Christian-site walks.

**Friday.** Mahane Yehuda peaks and then shuts by mid-afternoon. The Muslim Quarter fills for the noon prayer, and access lanes near the Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif gates are closed to non-worshippers for a few hours around it; the Mount itself is closed to visitors all day. Old City walks start early on Fridays for this reason.

**Yom Kippur** (late September or early October): the country stops for 25 hours; no tours, no transport, nothing open. **Passover and Sukkot** (spring and autumn): the intermediate days are the busiest tourist weeks of the year for Israelis, with sold-out Tunnels slots and long queues at the City of David. **Holy Week and Orthodox Easter**: the Holy Sepulchre is packed and parts of the Via Dolorosa are closed for processions; the Holy Fire ceremony on Orthodox Holy Saturday closes the Christian Quarter to anyone without a pass. **Ramadan**: Temple Mount visiting hours for non-Muslims shrink or are suspended, and the Muslim Quarter is lively after dark and quiet by day.

## Dress code and access at the holy sites

**Western Wall.** Shoulders and knees covered. Men cover their heads (paper kippot are provided); men and women pray in separate sections, and visitors of any faith may approach the wall. No photography or phones on Shabbat and Jewish holidays.

**Church of the Holy Sepulchre and other churches.** Shoulders and knees covered for men and women; hats off inside. Expect a queue of 30 to 90 minutes for the Edicule (the Tomb) at midday; early morning and late afternoon are quieter.

**Temple Mount / Haram al-Sharif.** Non-Muslim visitors enter only through the Mughrabi (Bab al-Maghariba) Gate, reached by the wooden ramp beside the Western Wall plaza, and only in limited windows on Sunday to Thursday mornings and early afternoons; the site is closed to visitors on Fridays, Saturdays and Muslim holidays, and hours change during Ramadan. Non-Muslims may not enter the Dome of the Rock or the Al-Aqsa Mosque, may not pray, and may not bring in religious objects. Dress is strictly modest: long trousers or skirts, covered shoulders, and women may be asked to put on a wrap, available at the gate. Expect an airport-style security check and a queue; arrive when the gate opens. Very few group walking tours include the Mount because of the timing, so plan it as a separate early-morning visit.

**Mosques and synagogues elsewhere** follow the same rules: modest dress, shoes off in mosques, head covering for men in synagogues.

## Free walking tours vs paid Jerusalem tours

Tip-based "free" walking tours of the Old City leave from Jaffa Gate daily; Sandemans New Europe is the best-known operator. The format is a two-hour highlights walk with a large group (often 25 to 40) and a guide who is paid by what participants choose to give at the end. You get the orientation and the main stops, not the Holy Sepulchre interior or the Western Wall plaza time that a paid half-day tour includes. For a first hour in the Old City, or on a tight budget, they work well; if you are going to Jerusalem once, the paid small-group walk is the better use of a morning. Jerusalem's municipal tourism office has also run free guided walks on Saturdays; ask at the Jaffa Gate information office, since the programme changes.

## Seeing Jerusalem without a tour

Jerusalem is one of the easiest cities in the country to do on your own if you have two days.

**Getting there.** From Tel Aviv, the fast train runs from HaHagana, HaShalom and Savidor stations to Jerusalem-Yitzhak Navon in about 35 to 40 minutes, several times an hour, Sunday to Thursday and on Friday mornings. From Navon, the light rail runs along Jaffa Road to City Hall (for Jaffa Gate) and Damascus Gate stops. Intercity buses from Tel Aviv reach Jerusalem's Central Bus Station, next to the train station. On Shabbat, shared taxis (sheruts) run between the two cities from Tel Aviv's HaHagana area. Ben Gurion Airport has a direct train to Navon. See our [first-time in Israel guide](/first-time-in-israel) for how the Rav-Kav transport card works.

**Doing the Old City alone.** Enter at Jaffa Gate, where the tourist information office has maps. The four quarters are walkable in a morning: Jaffa Gate to the Armenian Quarter, down to the Jewish Quarter and the Western Wall plaza, up through the Muslim Quarter's souk to the Via Dolorosa and the Holy Sepulchre, out through Damascus Gate. The main sites are free to enter. The Ramparts Walk (a small fee) is the best orientation and is rarely on group tours. Book the Western Wall Tunnels and City of David directly online.

**Beyond the walls.** The Mount of Olives is a taxi ride or a steep 20-minute walk from Lions' Gate; go early for the light. Yad Vashem, the Israel Museum and Mahane Yehuda are all on or near the light rail. Masada and the Dead Sea are a separate day, on a tour or by bus; see our [Masada visitor guide](/masada-visitor-guide) and [Dead Sea tours compared](/dead-sea-tours-compared).

**Where to stay** shapes all of this. A hotel within walking distance of Jaffa Gate or on the light rail line saves an hour a day; our [where to stay in Jerusalem](/best-hotels-jerusalem) guide compares the neighbourhoods. And if you are entering from abroad, check the [visa and entry rules](/visa-information) first.

## Is a guided Jerusalem tour worth it?

For the Old City on a first visit, yes. The history is dense, the traditions compete, the lanes confuse, and a good guide turns four hours of walking past things into four hours of understanding them. For everything outside the walls — the museums, the market, Yad Vashem — self-guiding works well and costs less. The pattern that suits most visitors is a guided Old City morning on day one, one or two booked sites (the Tunnels, the City of David) on day two, and the rest at your own pace.
