Hanukkah — the Festival of Lights — is one of the few Jewish holidays that looks outward rather than inward. While Yom Kippur is solemn and Shabbat is quiet, Hanukkah is public: hanukkiot in every window, jam doughnuts on every market stall, and an eight-night rhythm of candlelighting that turns the streets of Jerusalem, Tel Aviv, and Safed into something genuinely different. For a visitor, it is also one of the most accessible holidays to experience — no synagogue membership required, no Yom Tov closures to navigate, and prices that sit well below the summer or Sukkot peaks.

## Hanukkah dates

| Year | First candle | Eighth candle |
|---|---|---|
| 2026 | Evening of 4 December | 12 December |
| 2027 | Evening of 24 December | 1 January |

The first candle is lit at nightfall on the first evening; one candle is added each night until all eight burn together on the final night. In 2026, Hanukkah sits entirely within the school-holiday period (Israeli schools break for the full eight days), which means domestic family travel surges — particularly to Eilat and Jerusalem.

## What the holiday looks like

Hanukkah commemorates the rededication of the Second Temple after the Maccabee forces defeated the Seleucid army, and specifically the miracle of a single day's worth of oil burning for eight days in the Temple menorah. The holiday is post-biblical (rabbinically instituted), so it carries none of the biblical 'day of rest' restrictions. **Shops open. Buses run. National parks operate.** It is, in practical terms, a working week overlaid with a nightly ceremony.

What visitors actually see:

- **Hanukkiot in every window**: by law and custom, menorahs are placed at the entrance to homes or in windows facing the street — to publicise the miracle publicly (*pirsumei nissa*). After dark, the illuminated nine-branched candelabras appear in almost every Jewish household window across the country.
- **Sufganiyot everywhere**: the classic Hanukkah food is the sufganiyah — a fried jam doughnut, now available in dozens of flavours from basic strawberry jam to Nutella, lotus, salted caramel, and pistachio. Bakeries and market stalls begin selling them from early December. The smell of frying oil (echoing the miracle of the Temple oil) is inescapable and wonderful.
- **Latkes at restaurants**: potato pancakes (levivot in Hebrew) appear on restaurant menus throughout the holiday period; ask for them fried fresh.
- **Public lightings**: in addition to the private home lightings, major menorahs are lit publicly — in city squares, at the Western Wall, outside government buildings, and at shopping centres.

## Jerusalem: the heart of Hanukkah

Jerusalem is the city most saturated with Hanukkah feeling. The connection is direct — the Second Temple whose rededication Hanukkah marks stood on the Temple Mount, and the Maccabees' military campaign was fought in the Judean hills that surround the city.

### The Western Wall

The Western Wall plaza holds **large public menorah lightings on each of the eight nights**, usually beginning shortly after nightfall (around 17:00–17:30 in December). The first-night lighting draws particularly large crowds and often features musical performances and official state participation. There is no ticket or advance booking required. Dress warmly — Jerusalem in December is 8–14°C in the evenings.

### The Jewish Quarter, Old City

The Jewish Quarter's narrow stone streets are at their most atmospheric after nightfall during Hanukkah. Hanukkiot glow in windows and doorways along Cardo, the Broad Wall Promenade, and the side lanes of the quarter. The combination of ancient stone walls, candlelight, and the scent of sufganiyot from the bakeries near the Zion Gate area is unlike anywhere else. Join the many locals who walk the quarter each evening after the candlelighting.

### Tower of David Museum

The Tower of David Museum in the Jaffa Gate complex runs **special Hanukkah programming** each December, including extended hours for its acclaimed Night Spectacular light projection shows. Tickets sell out during the school-holiday week; book at least one to two weeks ahead through the Tower of David website. The evening approach to Jaffa Gate with the lit citadel is one of Jerusalem's most photogenic December moments. See the [Tower of David guide](/tower-of-david-guide) for visitor information and practical details.

### Mahane Yehuda Market

Jerusalem's central market becomes the city's best Hanukkah food destination. The shuk's covered alleys fill with sufganiyot stalls, the outdoor section buzzes with evening crowds, and many of the popular bars along the covered-market strip stay open late. It is the place to taste multiple sufganiyah varieties side by side and to see the market at its festive winter best. See the [Jerusalem food guide](/jerusalem-food-guide) for the best market stops and surrounding restaurant recommendations.

## Tel Aviv: urban Hanukkah

Tel Aviv observes Hanukkah more casually than Jerusalem but with its own character. The Tel Aviv municipality typically organises **public menorah lightings in Rabin Square or Dizengoff Square** — the city's main public gathering spaces — on one or more evenings of the holiday. Check the Tel Aviv municipality website closer to December 2026 for the current year's programme.

**Hilton Beach and the seafront promenade** sometimes host public menorah lightings organised by Chabad (an international Jewish outreach organisation), with the large outdoor hanukkiah positioned to be visible from the beach and the seafront boulevard. These events are open to all and draw a mix of Israelis and tourists.

The independent Tel Aviv bakery scene — particularly in the Florentin, Neve Tzedek, and Carmel Market neighbourhoods — produces some of the country's most creative sufganiyot. The Carmel Market itself (Shuk HaCarmel) is a worthwhile December morning visit for market atmosphere and street food before the holiday-week crowds peak in the afternoon.

## Safed (Tzfat): Hanukkah by candlelight

Safed, the mystical Kabbalistic city in the Upper Galilee, observes Hanukkah with particular depth. The Kabbalah tradition places great significance on the Hanukkah lights as a spiritual illumination — and Safed, with its centuries-old synagogues, its narrow lanes, and its artist quarter, becomes genuinely otherworldly after dark during the eight nights.

**Nightly Candle Tours**: local guides lead walking tours of the Old Jewish Quarter after nightfall during Hanukkah, visiting ancient synagogues for their candlelightings — including the Abuhav Synagogue and the Ha-Ari Ashkenazi Synagogue — and stopping at local homes whose mezuzah-framed doorways glow with hanukkiot. These tours are organised through local Safed tourism operators; book through the Safed Tourism Association or a guide listed on the Israel Ministry of Tourism's certified-guide database.

The walk through the Old Quarter under the December stars, with every window lit, is Safed at its most quietly spectacular. See the [Safed travel guide](/safed-tzfat-guide) for accommodation and access details — an overnight stay is strongly recommended over a day trip.

## The Hanukkah Torch Relay

A modern Israeli Hanukkah tradition with deep symbolic weight, the **Moreshet HaMaccabim Torch Relay** begins at Modiʿin — the Maccabees' ancestral home in the Judean foothills between Jerusalem and Tel Aviv — and carries a flame by relay runners through central Israel, ending in a public ceremony in Jerusalem. The relay has been an annual state event since 1944, predating the State of Israel by four years.

The relay is a working model of the Maccabees' route from Modiʿin to Jerusalem and is broadcast nationally. Viewing points along the relay route and the final ceremony location vary year to year; check the Israeli government's official Hanukkah portal and the Jerusalem municipality website closer to December 2026 for the current year's arrangements.

## Practical logistics

### What is open during Hanukkah

Everything. Hanukkah has no Yom Tov restrictions. Public transport runs on normal schedules. National parks (Masada, Ein Gedi, the Golan sites) are open and operating — though school-holiday crowds mean arriving early at popular sites is advisable on weekdays, and weekend days in Hanukkah week will be genuinely busy.

### The Shabbat overlap

The only operational complication is Shabbat. In 2026, **December 4 (first candle night) falls on a Friday evening and December 5 is a Saturday** — the first night of Hanukkah coincides with the start of Shabbat. On Friday December 4, Shabbat candles are lit before the Hanukkah hanukkiah; public transport stops at sunset; Jewish-owned restaurants and shops close from Friday afternoon through Saturday evening. Plan accordingly if you arrive on the first day of Hanukkah. The remaining seven nights in 2026 fall on Sunday through Saturday, with the following Shabbat (December 11/12) coinciding with the final night — the same rules apply. See the [Shabbat guide](/shabbat-guide) for what to expect on Shabbat evenings anywhere in Israel.

### Prices and booking

December in Israel is low season for international visitors — outside Christmas week (December 23–January 2), hotels are among the year's cheapest, and major sites have shorter queues than any other time of year. Hanukkah week (December 4–12 in 2026) introduces a **domestic family-travel surge** that tightens supply in Eilat and popular Jerusalem areas, but prices remain well below Sukkot or Passover levels. Book 4–6 weeks ahead for any family-friendly hotel in the school-holiday destinations (Eilat, Jerusalem Old City-adjacent, Galilee kibbutz resorts).

### Getting around

The Hanukkah period is a good window for renting a car — national parks and Galilee sites are accessible, and without Yom Tov restrictions there are no multi-day transport gaps to plan around. See the [car rental Israel guide](/car-rental-israel) for agency recommendations and Shabbat logistics.
