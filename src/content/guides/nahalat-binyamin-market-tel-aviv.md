**Nahalat Binyamin** is Tel Aviv's oldest artisan market, operating along a quiet pedestrian mall in the heart of the city since the 1920s. Unlike the chaotic energy of the adjacent Carmel Market, the Nahalat Binyamin experience is quieter, more personal, and fundamentally different in character: two hundred juried stalls, each run by the Israeli artist or craftsperson who made the items on display.

The market opens twice a week — Tuesdays and Fridays only — and the quality control is strict. Every vendor is vetted by a committee; only original, handmade work is permitted. The effect is a craft market that genuinely reflects Israeli creative culture rather than generic tourist souvenirs.

---

## What makes Nahalat Binyamin different

Most craft markets blend handmade items with imported goods or workshop-produced pieces. Nahalat Binyamin enforces a rule that distinguishes it from nearly every other market in Israel: **the person selling the item made the item.** A ceramicist at the wheel. A goldsmith who trained in Jerusalem's apprenticeship workshops. A printmaker who has spent 30 years on one recurring Jerusalem motif.

The result is a browsing experience with depth. You can ask any vendor exactly how a piece was made, where the materials came from, how long they have been working in this medium. Most will talk at length; some have been selling at the same Nahalat Binyamin stall for decades.

The market committee reviews applications and removes vendors who import goods or use industrial production. This is enforced, not aspirational — it is what separates the quality you find here from the Old City souvenir stalls or general import shops.

## What you will find

### Jewellery

Israeli jewellery design at Nahalat Binyamin draws on three distinct traditions: Mediterranean gold and silver work with filigree patterns, Kabbalistic and religious symbolism (Hamsa, Tree of Life, Star of David reinterpreted in contemporary form), and secular modernist design influenced by the Bezalel Academy of Arts tradition. You will find all three at different stalls.

Price ranges typically run from simple silver-stud earrings (₪60–120) to statement necklaces and rings with semi-precious stones (₪300–800). Custom commissions are possible with many vendors — bring a reference if you have something specific in mind.

### Ceramics

Ceramic work at the market ranges from functional tableware — hand-thrown bowls and mugs with characteristic Israeli-desert glazes — to sculptural pieces inspired by biblical archaeology. Hamsas in glazed ceramic, Jerusalem landscape tile panels, and desert-palette vases are among the most commonly sold items. Work baked in a wood-fire kiln at a few stalls produces a distinctive ash-glaze finish unusual in the region.

### Original paintings and prints

Original oil and watercolour paintings of Israeli landscapes — Galilee hills, Jaffa port, Negev desert, Sea of Galilee — dominate the painting stalls. Prices depend heavily on size: small originals from ₪200–500, larger canvases from ₪600–2,500. Signed limited-edition prints of popular compositions run ₪80–250, making them the most practical take-home item in this category. Verify whether prints are original-edition limited runs or mass reproductions — the better vendors will show you the edition number and sign on request.

### Glasswork

Blown and fused glass is among the most visually striking work at the market: vivid colours, often sea and desert-inspired, in the form of vases, decorative objects, jewellery and Judaica items. Heavy pieces may present a packing challenge — most serious vendors have cardboard and bubble wrap, but ask before buying.

### Textiles and leather

Hand-dyed silk scarves, embroidered linen, leather wallets and bags made by craftspeople trained in traditional techniques appear across several stalls. Israeli embroidery patterns draw on Palestinian and Yemenite needlework traditions — the cross-cultural textile history of the region is visible in the thread work.

### Wooden Judaica

Hand-carved mezuzah cases, Shabbat candlestick holders, and decorative wooden frames using olive wood (from the Galilee and Judean foothills) are a consistent presence. Olive wood is dense, fine-grained and takes carving well; pieces are often scented with the wood's natural oils for years after purchase.

## When to go and how long to allow

**Friday mornings** are the peak Nahalat Binyamin experience — the full complement of vendors, the adjacent [Carmel Market](/tel-aviv-carmel-market) at maximum energy, and the surrounding Kerem HaTeimanim neighbourhood at its most alive. Arrive by 10am for best vendor presence and a calmer start; the space fills by noon.

**Tuesday mornings** offer the same vendors with fewer crowds. If your schedule is flexible, Tuesday between 10am and 12:30pm is the optimal window for unhurried browsing and conversation with vendors.

Allow **90 minutes to 2 hours** for the market itself if you are genuinely browsing; more if you want to talk with specific vendors. Combined with the Carmel Market next door, a full morning takes 3–4 hours.

The market is **outdoors and exposed** — there is minimal shade on the Nahalat Binyamin pedestrian mall. In summer (June–September), hat, sunscreen and water are recommended. In winter (December–February), some stalls shelter under awnings but others pack up in heavy rain.

## Combining Nahalat Binyamin with Carmel Market

The two markets run simultaneously on Tuesdays and Fridays and are separated by a single block. The natural routing is to start at the **northern end of Nahalat Binyamin** (entering from Allenby), browse south through the craft stalls, exit onto HaCarmel Street, and enter Carmel Market from its Allenby end. This takes you through the food-and-spice northern half of Carmel Market toward King George Street.

Or reverse: start at the **Carmel Market north entrance** (King George Street) with the produce and street food, work south, and finish with the Nahalat Binyamin craft stalls before exiting toward Allenby. This order is better if you want food before crafts.

Friday timing matters: Carmel Market's produce stalls close around **2pm for Shabbat**. Plan to finish food shopping by 1:30pm if you want both markets. Nahalat Binyamin also wraps up in the early-to-mid afternoon on Fridays.

## Getting there

Nahalat Binyamin Street runs between Allenby Street and Gruzenberg Street in central Tel Aviv, one block west of the Carmel Market and immediately south of the Kerem HaTeimanim neighbourhood.

From **Rothschild Boulevard** (the Bauhaus district), it is a 10-minute walk south along Rothschild then left onto Nahalat Binyamin.

The **Tel Aviv Red Line light rail** stops at Allenby (within a 5-minute walk) and Rothschild. Both are practical drop-off points for this area.

**Rideshare** (Gett, Yango) is fast and inexpensive within central Tel Aviv. Drop off at the Allenby/Nahalat Binyamin junction.

**Parking** near the market is impractical during session hours — the streets are dense and the area is pedestrianised. The [car rental guide](/car-rental-israel) is relevant if you are driving in from outside the city, but once in central Tel Aviv, leave the car at your accommodation.

## Practical tips

**Payment:** Cash is accepted everywhere; card (contactless) acceptance varies by vendor. Bring cash in small denominations — most vendors struggle to make change for ₪200 notes. ATMs are available on nearby Allenby and King George Streets.

**Language:** Most vendors speak functional English. Hebrew will get you a warmer response, but it is not required for a full market experience.

**Packing:** For fragile purchases — glass, ceramics — ask the vendor about packing. Most serious vendors carry bubble wrap; some charge a small amount for careful multi-layer wrapping. Checked baggage is a safer bet than carry-on for breakable items on a return flight.

**Shabbat:** The market does not operate on Saturdays. If you arrive in Tel Aviv on a Friday and plan to shop, get to the market by 10–11am — vendors begin closing early as Shabbat approaches in the afternoon. See the [Shabbat guide](/shabbat-guide) for what to expect in the city on Friday evenings and Saturdays.

**Combine with:** the [Tel Aviv food guide](/tel-aviv-food-guide), the [3-day Tel Aviv itinerary](/3-days-in-tel-aviv) (the market is the natural Day 1 or Day 2 morning anchor), and the [Neve Tzedek neighbourhood](/tel-aviv-neighborhoods-guide) immediately south — a 15-minute walk from the market into one of Tel Aviv's most photogenic streets.

## Beyond the market: the Kerem HaTeimanim neighbourhood

The streets surrounding Nahalat Binyamin form the **Kerem HaTeimanim** (Yemenite Quarter) neighbourhood — Tel Aviv's oldest surviving residential neighbourhood, built by Yemenite Jewish immigrants in the early 20th century. The low stone houses, narrow alleys and Yemenite-heritage restaurants give this pocket of the city a character entirely different from the Bauhaus White City or the beachfront.

The food in Kerem HaTeimanim deserves its own visit: **jachnun** (slow-baked Yemenite pastry, traditionally served Saturday morning), **malawach** (flaky pan-fried flatbread), and Yemenite meat soups are available at several small restaurants along Kerem HaTeimanim Street and the surrounding lanes. Most are cash-only and open for lunch; evenings are quieter.

A Friday morning route that combines the market, a Yemenite lunch, and a late-afternoon walk through Neve Tzedek is one of the more distinctive ways to spend time in Tel Aviv.
