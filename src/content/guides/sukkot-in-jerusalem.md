Jerusalem is the heart of Sukkot. The holiday was created with this city in mind — ancient pilgrims travelled to the Temple three times a year, and Sukkot was the grandest of those pilgrimages. The Temple is gone, but the Western Wall remains, and during Sukkot tens of thousands still converge on its plaza at dawn for the Birkat Kohanim: Kohanim spreading their tallitot, the crowd filling the stones below, and the threefold blessing from Numbers echoing across two millennia of unbroken tradition. For a visitor, there is nothing quite like it in the world.

Jerusalem during Sukkot is also, practically speaking, the most in-demand place in Israel. **Old City hotels sell out two to three months ahead.** Prices spike. National parks and major sites are mobbed by domestic tourists. Knowing how to navigate the week — what to book, what to arrive early for, what to skip, and when to slow down — makes the difference between a frustrating holiday and a genuinely extraordinary one.

This is worth noting for logistics: Friday afternoon through Saturday night is the quietest stretch of the holiday.

## The Western Wall Birkat Kohanim

The **Birkat Kohanim** (Priestly Blessing) is the single most extraordinary public event in Jerusalem's holiday calendar. During Sukkot and Passover, Kohanim — Jews tracing their descent from the Temple's priestly caste — gather in the Western Wall plaza, drape their tallitot over their heads, and recite the ancient threefold blessing from Numbers 6:24–26 over the tens of thousands assembled in the plaza below. The scale is overwhelming: the whole plaza is packed before dawn, the Kohanim form rows under their prayer shawls, and the words carry across the same stones the Temple priests once walked.

**What to know to attend:**

- **No ticket or advance registration is required** — the Western Wall plaza is open to all respectful visitors
- **Arrive by 6:30am at the latest.** The plaza begins filling from 5:00am; positions are gone by 7:00am
- The ceremony is part of the Shacharit morning prayer service; the Birkat Kohanim portion begins around **7:30–8:00am**
- Security lines at the Old City gates can be long on Sukkot mornings — allow **30 minutes** from Jaffa Gate or Dung Gate
- **Dress modestly**: covered shoulders and knees for men and women; free kippot and head scarves are available at the entrance
- Men's and women's sections are in operation on both sides of the mehitzah; both have good sightlines to the plaza
- **The exact Chol HaMoed morning** for Birkat Kohanim shifts each year — confirm in the days before your visit at the Western Wall Foundation website or by asking your hotel

## The Jerusalem March

The **Jerusalem March** (also known as the Sukkot International Parade) is one of the holiday's most colourful events and one that is often unknown to first-time visitors. On one of the Chol HaMoed days, tens of thousands of participants from more than 100 countries — Christian pilgrims, Messianic groups, evangelical church delegations, and Israeli Jewish marchers — process through the main streets of Jerusalem carrying national flags, singing, and dancing.

Three route lengths are available — approximately 5, 7, and 10 kilometres — running through the New City and past the walls of the Old City. The parade is organised by the International Christian Embassy Jerusalem (ICEJ) and is free to join; registration opens through the ICEJ website (feast.icej.org) several weeks before Sukkot. It is well suited to families with older children.

Even as a spectator, the Jerusalem March is worth planning for: the route passes through central streets and can affect traffic. If you have a car, plan your morning driving around the parade; check the municipality website for road-closure timings.

## Tower of David during Sukkot

The **Tower of David Museum and Citadel**, inside the Jaffa Gate complex, is one of Jerusalem's best Sukkot destinations. The museum runs extended programming during the holiday week, including:

- **Night Spectacular** — the acclaimed sound-and-light show projected onto the ancient citadel walls, extended for additional performances during Sukkot week
- Extended daytime hours throughout Chol HaMoed
- Special exhibitions and children's programming during the school-holiday period

**Important**: Night Spectacular tickets sell out weeks ahead of Sukkot. Book through the Tower of David website before you arrive in Jerusalem — do not assume availability. Daytime entry can usually be purchased on arrival but is significantly crowded; pre-booking saves time. See the [Tower of David guide](/tower-of-david-guide) for full ticketing details, access, and the permanent exhibition.

## The Old City during Sukkot

Jerusalem's **Old City** at Sukkot is the most immersive neighbourhood in Israel during the holiday. What changes during the week:

**Jewish Quarter**: sukkot appear on balconies, in courtyard restaurants, and outside homes throughout the Quarter. The narrow stone lanes fill with families, the bakeries produce special Sukkot pastries, and the covered sukkah seating at restaurants makes for a vivid and festive meal setting. Cardo and the Broad Wall Promenade are particularly atmospheric in the evenings.

**Mahane Yehuda (the Shuk)**: the market is packed with lulav and etrog sellers in the final week before Sukkot and festive with outdoor sukkah seating and evening energy during Chol HaMoed. Many of the market's popular bars and restaurants are open until late. Arrive in the morning to beat the worst of the crowds.

**Practical note on restaurants**: many Jerusalem restaurants build a sukkah for the week and seat guests inside it throughout Chol HaMoed. On the first day (September 26) and Shemini Atzeret (October 3), most Jewish-owned restaurants close. If you plan to eat out on those days, confirm with your hotel for kosher restaurants that may be open, or prepare by stocking up the day before.

## The Abu Gosh Music Festival

The **Abu Gosh Music Festival** is one of Jerusalem area's best-kept Sukkot secrets. Since 1980, the festival has presented classical vocal and chamber music concerts in the **Benedictine Abbey of the Resurrection** at Abu Gosh — a 12th-century Crusader church with stone vaulting that produces extraordinary natural acoustics. Abu Gosh is a 20-minute drive west of Jerusalem (or an easy sherut from the Central Bus Station).

The Sukkot weekend programme typically includes five to six concerts over three days, featuring international soloists and Israeli chamber ensembles. Tickets sell out weeks ahead; book through the Abu Gosh festival website (abugoshfestival.co.il) well in advance. The combination of the acoustics, the ancient stone setting, and an Israeli audience dressed for a holiday evening makes it an experience genuinely unlike any concert hall.

## Chol HaMoed day trips from Jerusalem

The five intermediate Chol HaMoed days are when the whole of Israel is moving, and Jerusalem is the natural base. Popular day trips:

**Masada and the Dead Sea**: the classic Chol HaMoed combination. Israel's most visited archaeological site pairs with a float in the Dead Sea. **Arrive at Masada before 7:00am** — the cable car queue reaches 45–60 minutes by mid-morning during Chol HaMoed. Aim to be back in the car and heading north by 11:00am before Dead Sea beach crowds peak. See the [Dead Sea guide](/dead-sea) for beach access and safe swimming.

**Beit Guvrin / Maresha**: the ancient cave city 45 minutes south-west of Jerusalem is a striking Chol HaMoed option — less crowded than the Dead Sea, genuinely fascinating archaeologically, and easily combined with a wine-tasting lunch in the Judean Hills. National park entry is included in the Israel Nature and Parks Authority Sukkot combo ticket.

**Ein Gedi Nature Reserve**: the desert oasis on the Dead Sea shore is another classic Chol HaMoed destination — David Stream (Nahal David) and Arugot Stream trails, ibex, hyrax, and a waterfall. Combined with a Dead Sea float, it makes a full day. Arrive early; both streams close to new entry once visitor numbers reach the daily cap.

## Where to stay in Jerusalem for Sukkot

**Book two to three months ahead.** Jerusalem's most sought-after Sukkot accommodation is gone by July or August for a September holiday. The areas and properties that sell out first:

**Old City and Mamilla**: the Mamilla Hotel (above the Jaffa Gate area), boutique guesthouses inside the Jewish Quarter and Armenian Quarter, and the Notre Dame Guesthouse are the fastest to sell out. Old City access on Yom Tov days (when public transport stops) is enormously easier if your accommodation is a walking distance from the Western Wall.

**City Centre / King David Street**: the King David Hotel, David Citadel, and Waldorf Astoria fill early. These properties also carry all-inclusive Yom Tov packages covering meals for the closed days — if you are observant or want logistics simplified, these packages are worth considering.

**German Colony and Katamon**: slightly further from the Old City but with good value and neighbourhood atmosphere; 15–20 minutes on foot to the Jaffa Gate, or a short taxi.

**Prices**: expect 30–50% above shoulder-season rates during Sukkot week. A room that costs ₪600–800 in September outside the holiday may run ₪900–1,200 during Sukkot. Apartments through Booking.com or Airbnb are often better value for families, particularly if you are self-catering through the Yom Tov days.

## Getting around Jerusalem during Sukkot

**Public transport**: Jerusalem's light rail and buses stop on the first day, Shabbat, and Shemini Atzeret/Simchat Torah. During Chol HaMoed (and the Sukkot Friday/Shabbat combination), plan accordingly — the first day of Sukkot (September 26, 2026) is both Yom Tov and Shabbat, so transport stops from Friday afternoon through Saturday night. The light rail runs normally on the Chol HaMoed weekdays.

**Traffic**: Jerusalem is heavily congested during Chol HaMoed, particularly on the approach roads to the Old City and the Begin Highway. If you are driving, arrive at major sites by 8:00am to secure parking, or use the Jaffa Gate parking garage (book ahead; fills by 9:00am during Chol HaMoed). The Jerusalem light rail is the better option for Old City access on non-Yom Tov days.

**Car hire**: useful for day trips to Masada, Ein Gedi, and Abu Gosh on Chol HaMoed weekdays. On Yom Tov days, driving is permissible for non-Jewish visitors but roads into central Jerusalem are quiet and parking is easy — the city itself is effectively car-free.
